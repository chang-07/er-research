\begin{abstract}
Risk, compliance, and reference-data teams must follow a legal
entity through mergers, rebrands, spinoffs, and jurisdiction
changes, where its name and identifiers change by design. Snapshot
entity-resolution benchmarks do not pose this task, and the open
registry that records such events (GLEIF) does so only as sparse,
partly administrative successor links. We introduce
\textsc{Sylvester}, a public benchmark and empirical study of
\emph{lifecycle-aware} entity resolution: 125{,}519 labeled pairs
over 3.3M entities from GLEIF Level-1/2, SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata,
with held-out time, event-type, and jurisdiction splits. Because
successors change names while hard negatives are mined to be
name-similar, name similarity is anti-predictive on the released
pool, so we evaluate every method on three candidate pools: the
released pool, an unrelated miner's pool, and retrieval over the
full universe. Methods that exploit the construction (inverted name
similarity, 64--70\% Hits@1 on time/event; a name-feature
gradient-boosted matcher, 0.73--0.84) collapse off-pool. Among
name-only methods, a 22M-parameter cross-encoder transfers
(57--61\% Hits@1 on time and event), 24--35\,pp ahead of the best
bi-encoder and ahead of six zero-shot LLMs. Input matters as much
as architecture: given the full registry record, a gradient-boosted
matcher beats that cross-encoder on-pool by 18\,pp, and a
cross-encoder over the same record wins the fielded setting by
12\,pp (both $p<10^{-4}$). Even so, the best system reaches only
21--39\% Hits@1 end to end, because first-stage retrieval
(Recall@20 of 33--50\%) misses most true successors: the
bottleneck is finding the candidate, not scoring it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Twitter Inc.\ became X Corp. Facebook became Meta. Sprint was
absorbed into T-Mobile. Each pair shares almost nothing on the
surface (different legal names and tickers, sometimes different
jurisdictions and legal forms), yet describes one continuing
economic entity. We ask: \emph{given two records observed at
different times, do they describe the same economic entity,
accounting for any lifecycle event between them?}

\paragraph{Who needs this, and why it matters.} Several groups do
this linkage routinely. Risk teams aggregate counterparty exposure
by legal entity, and a stale identifier splits one exposure in two.
Compliance teams screen clients against sanctions and KYC records,
where a renamed or absorbed entity must still match.
Reference-data teams maintain security masters and corporate-action
histories, and researchers need the same links to build firm-level
panels free of survivorship bias. The costly error is the same in
each case: one entity treated as two, or a successor never found,
and pipelines that make it fail silently.

\paragraph{Why it is hard.} Snapshot entity resolution (ER) relies
on surface similarity and does it well: LLM matchers reach 99\% F1
on point-in-time records~\cite{opensanctions2026}. Lifecycle events
remove that signal. A successor often inherits an acquirer's name
and can share no tokens with its predecessor
(Figure~\ref{fig:task}), while the most confusable candidates are
namesakes, siblings, and parents that share the name but are
legally distinct. Events are skewed (absorption is 94.5\% of our
held-out-event test set), and in deployment the successor must
first be retrieved from millions of entities.

\paragraph{Why no benchmark existed.} Commercial reference-data
vendors maintain lifecycle links, but their data is licensed and
cannot be redistributed. The open GLEIF registry holds the raw
pieces but not a benchmark. Its \texttt{SuccessorEntity} links are
filed voluntarily, only 67\% resolve to a registered successor,
5.4\% of those are administrative re-registrations, and the event
log was sparse before 2022 (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}). Its Level-2
relationship records encode ownership and fund hierarchy, not
succession: only 0.1\% of our positives have a Level-2 edge
between predecessor and successor (\S\ref{ssec:sources}). A
benchmark also needs hard negatives, held-out splits, and a full
candidate universe, which the registry does not provide, and
existing ER benchmarks are snapshot-framed (\S\ref{sec:related}).

\paragraph{This paper.} We build that benchmark and use it for an
empirical study. The models are standard on purpose: string
baselines, gradient-boosted matchers, off-the-shelf sentence-encoder
bi- and cross-encoders, and six zero-shot LLMs. The contribution is
the benchmark and what it reveals. One design choice shapes the
results: negatives are mined name-similar while successors are
name-different, which targets the sub-problem left once a
name-aware retriever has narrowed the field (\emph{which
name-similar candidate is the true successor?}) but also makes the
pool exploitable. We therefore score every method on three
candidate pools and judge it by its worst.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An open benchmark} (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}):
        125{,}519 labeled pairs over 3.3M GLEIF entities, four
        hard-negative classes, held-out time, event-type, and
        jurisdiction splits, and a Wikidata marquee supplement.

  \item \textbf{A three-pool diagnostic}
        (\S\ref{ssec:anti}, \S\ref{ssec:robustness}): the released
        pool, an unrelated miner's pool, and retrieval over the full
        universe. Methods that exploit the construction post the best
        name-input scores on the released pool and collapse on the
        other two.

  \item \textbf{Empirical findings} (\S\ref{sec:experiments}):
        among name-only methods, a 22M-parameter cross-encoder is the
        one that transfers, 24--35\,pp Hits@1 ahead of the best
        bi-encoder and ahead of six zero-shot LLMs on TIME and EVENT.
        The full structured record matters as much as architecture:
        a gradient-boosted matcher over it beats that cross-encoder
        on-pool by 18\,pp, and a cross-encoder over it wins the
        fielded pool by 12\,pp. Retrieval, not reranking, is the
        deployment bottleneck: Recall@20 of 33--50\% caps end-to-end
        Hits@1 at 21--39\%, echoing the blocking literature's focus
        on candidate generation~\cite{uniblocker2024}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Classical entity resolution matches records by similarity over
structured fields~\cite{fellegisunter1969}, and modern PLM
cross-encoders on serialized records (Ditto~\cite{ditto2021},
GraLMatch~\cite{gralmatch2025}) are now standard; the OpenSanctions
Pairs corpus~\cite{opensanctions2026} benchmarks LLM matching at
755K pairs. All of this work adopts a \emph{snapshot} framing
rather than lifecycle-spanning matches. Closest in spirit is
temporal record linkage~\cite{temporalrl2011}, which pioneered
matching drifting entities but predates modern encoders and open
registries. AnyMatch~\cite{anymatch2024} shows that small fine-tuned models rival LLM
matchers at a fraction of the cost, and WDC
Products~\cite{wdcproducts2024} studies how hard-negative
construction shapes benchmark difficulty, the axis
\S\ref{ssec:anti} examines here. Finally, TempEL~\cite{tempel2022}
time-stratifies entity linking against an evolving Wikipedia, but
its task is mention-to-KB disambiguation rather than
record-to-record matching over legal entities, and
financial-filing knowledge graphs (FinReflectKG~\cite{finreflectkg2025},
from this conference) are built at a single time slice. None of them poses the lifecycle-bridge question directly.

\section{The Sylvester Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Data sources}
\label{ssec:sources}

We construct \textsc{Sylvester} entirely from open, persistently-licensed
sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-1 LEI-CDF.} The LEI repository (3.3M
        entities, 2026-05-19 snapshot), CC0, refreshed every eight
        hours. Each \texttt{LEIRecord} carries legal name,
        jurisdiction, legal form, addresses, registration metadata, a
        dated \texttt{LegalEntityEvents} log, and a
        \texttt{SuccessorEntity} field linking predecessor to
        successor by LEI.

  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-2 Relationship Records.} 473{,}839 typed
        inter-LEI relationships, used in hard-negative construction.

  \item \textbf{Wikidata.} High-profile M\&A via SPARQL on P156
        (\emph{followed by}); a marquee supplement and
        cross-validation source.

  \item \textbf{SEC EDGAR.} \texttt{company\_tickers.json} (10{,}354
        records) gives a name$\leftrightarrow$CIK crosswalk to GLEIF
        for the U.S.\ public subset (3{,}334 confident links);
        released with the benchmark, not used in the evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What GLEIF Level-2 already provides.} Level-2 records
have six types: direct and ultimate accounting consolidation, fund
management, sub-fund, feeder fund, and international branch. All
describe who owns or manages whom; none records that one entity
became another. In the same snapshot, 34 of our 31{,}824 positives
(0.1\%) have any Level-2 edge between predecessor and successor, 74
(0.2\%) share a Level-2 parent, and only 0.6\% of predecessors
appear in Level-2 at all. Level-2 therefore supplies
related-but-distinct \emph{negatives} here, not positives. The raw
positives are Level-1 \texttt{SuccessorEntity} links;
\textsc{Sylvester} adds what they lack: resolution to registered
successors, removal of administrative links, hard negatives,
held-out splits, and alternative candidate pools.

\subsection{Positive labels}

Our gold-standard positives are GLEIF \texttt{SuccessorEntity}
links: explicit predecessor-LEI $\to$ successor-LEI pairs maintained
by GLEIF's network of Local Operating Units. The 2026-05-19 snapshot
contains 50{,}202 records with a non-null \texttt{SuccessorEntity}
element, 33{,}645 of which resolve to a \texttt{SuccessorLEI}. The
rest point to successors that never registered an LEI, and we
exclude them.

\paragraph{Label quality validation.} A five-class auto-classifier on
the successor pairs (using documented event types, normalized name
delta, jurisdiction, legal form) yields: \texttt{strong\_real}
(documented event + name change) 25{,}428;
\texttt{likely\_real\_name\_changed} 3{,}330;
\texttt{likely\_real\_event\_only} 1{,}704; \texttt{ambiguous}
1{,}355; \textbf{\texttt{likely\_administrative}} 1{,}828 (5.4\%,
filtered out). We retain the first four classes (94.6\%;
\texttt{ambiguous} stays because every link is still LOU-curated),
yielding 31{,}817 plausibly-real predecessors (31{,}824 positive
rows; seven links are duplicated in the source export).

\subsection{Hard negatives}

For each positive predecessor we mine up to 80 hard negatives drawn from
four classes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (221 pairs) --- LEIs that share a
        Level-2 relationship-record edge (consolidation,
        fund-management, sub-fund) with the
        predecessor. These look related but are legally distinct.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} (10{,}208) --- distinct LEIs with
        identical normalized legal names in the same jurisdiction:
        coincidental name collision vs.\ true lifecycle linkage.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (10{,}703) --- distinct LEIs
        with identical normalized name in a different country.
  \item \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (232{,}601) --- fuzzy
        fallback: distinct LEIs in the same country sharing $\geq 2$
        content tokens after normalization, ranked by Jaccard. Covers the
        long tail when name-exact collisions are sparse.
\end{itemize}

After deduplication this leaves 253{,}733 candidate
(predecessor, hard-negative) pairs covering 20{,}192 of 31{,}817
predecessors, or roughly 63\%. The remaining 37\% are small
entities in jurisdictions without name collisions, and they fall
back to random in-batch negatives during training.

\subsection{Evaluation splits}
\label{ssec:splits}

For each positive we identify its \emph{anchor lifecycle event} --- the
highest-priority COMPLETED event on the predecessor's record by
type-priority then effective date. We then assign each positive (and its
associated negatives) to one of three split regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-out time:} anchor effective date $<$ 2025-01-01
        (train) vs.\ $\geq$ 2025-01-01 (test); completed-event
        volume rises $\approx$4$\times$ in 2022 when GLEIF tightened
        reporting, so the effective window is 2022--2026. Test:
        3{,}448 positives, 2{,}007 Hits@1-eligible anchors
        (\S\ref{ssec:metrics}).
  \item \textbf{Held-out event type:} train on M\&A,
        rename, legal-form change; test on spinoff, demerger,
        breakup, absorption --- dominated by \emph{absorption}
        (2{,}609 of 2{,}760 anchors, 94.5\%), the rest
        statistically thin (\S\ref{ssec:per_event}).
  \item \textbf{Held-out jurisdiction:} train on US + GB + DE; test on
        SG + JP + BR. Test composition: 161 anchors, 161 positives, of which
        88 are Hits@1-eligible --- the smallest split; its headline numbers carry
        ${\sim}{\pm}8.5$\,pp 95\% bootstrap CIs (vs.\
        ${\sim}{\pm}2.0$--$2.3$\,pp on time/event).
\end{itemize}

We refer to these regimes as TIME, EVENT, and JURIS.
Each positive gets up to 5 hard negatives if its splits are all
\texttt{train}, or up to 10 if any split places it in \texttt{test}, yielding
125{,}519 final pair rows.


\paragraph{Wikidata marquee supplement.} Released alongside the
benchmark: 302 high-profile M\&A pairs from Wikidata P156
both-sides-resolved to GLEIF, 283 not captured by GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} (Sprint\,$\to$\,T-Mobile,
Wyeth\,$\to$\,Pfizer, Interbrew\,$\to$\,AB-InBev); not used in the
headline evaluation.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:method}

The neural models are standard sentence encoders, used off the
shelf so that results speak to the benchmark rather than to a new
architecture. A bi-encoder (\S\ref{ssec:bi}) embeds each side
independently and scores by cosine, which suits retrieval. A
cross-encoder (\S\ref{ssec:ce}) reads both records jointly, which
suits reranking a short list and lets it compare specific tokens
across records whose surface form has changed.

\subsection{Bi-encoder}
\label{ssec:bi}

We fine-tune \texttt{intfloat/e5-small-v2} (33M params, 384-dim) via
supervised contrastive learning with state features folded into the
text prompt: \texttt{"query: } $n_\ell$ \texttt{| jurisdiction: }
$c_\ell$ \texttt{| form: } $f_\ell$\texttt{"}. Training uses the
26{,}297 positives in the strict no-leakage intersection of all
three split regimes (one hard neg per anchor), MultipleNegativesRanking
loss (InfoNCE; in-batch + explicit hard negs), 3 epochs, batch 32,
AdamW lr $2\times10^{-5}$, $\approx$6 min on Apple silicon.
Ablation variants (in-batch-only; E5-cosine hard negs plus 1{,}372
transitive positives) are in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}. The
bi-encoder uses contrastive loss and the cross-encoder BCE on the
logit --- different objectives by architectural convention.

\subsection{Cross-encoder}
\label{ssec:ce}

We fine-tune a second-stage cross-encoder that ingests
$(\text{record}_A, \text{record}_B)$ as a single concatenated sequence
and emits a single relevance logit. The backbone is the
22M-parameter \texttt{ms-marco-\allowbreak MiniLM-L-6-v2} cross-encoder
from HuggingFace; training uses the same positives as
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} (26{,}297 plus 1{,}372 transitives) plus a 4:1 sample of E5-cosine hard
negatives per positive, with positives labeled $1.0$ and negatives
$0.0$.
Loss: standard binary cross-entropy on the logit. 2 epochs, batch 32, lr
$2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup steps. The extensions in
\S\ref{ssec:main} hold architecture/optimizer constant and modify only
the serialized text, the training positives, and the backbone.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} Scoring all 125{,}519 pairs takes
$\approx$80 seconds on MPS; in deployment the cross-encoder
re-ranks a retriever's top-$K$, and the benchmark's fixed
5--10-candidate pools make direct full scoring feasible
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

\paragraph{Hard-negative re-mining.}
\label{ssec:hard_negs}
Re-mining with E5-cosine top-20 same-country candidates, in place of
the noisy token-overlap fallback, yields 652{,}764 negatives across
31{,}714 predecessors (vs.\ 253{,}733 over $\approx$20K before). The
cleaner negatives are what the cross-encoder trains on, to large
effect, and they are half of what later unlocks the full-record
GBM's transfer (\S\ref{ssec:anti}). For the bi-encoder they change
nothing (\S\ref{ssec:ablations}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The analyses follow the challenges in \S1: whether methods exploit
the name-similar pool (\S\ref{ssec:anti}, \S\ref{ssec:robustness}),
how they handle skewed event types (\S\ref{ssec:per_event}), and
whether they survive retrieval at registry scale
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}).

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four similarity baselines ---
\texttt{exact\_name} (normalized name equality), \texttt{token\_set}
(token Jaccard), \texttt{tfidf\_char} (char-3gram TF-IDF cosine),
and \texttt{sentence\_encoder} (zero-shot E5-small-v2, the method's
backbone un-tuned) --- and one \emph{supervised} classical matcher,
\texttt{features\_gbm}: gradient-boosted trees over twelve
string-similarity and field-agreement features in the
Fellegi--Sunter lineage~\cite{fellegisunter1969}, trained on the
released pool's no-leakage rows; the classical name-feature
default (\S\ref{ssec:anti} upgrades it to the full record). Six
zero-shot LLMs run under one protocol: one call per pair,
byte-identical prompts carrying the same name+state inputs as the
neural models (legal name, jurisdiction, legal form), a 0--10
integer rubric, \texttt{temperature=0} where exposed. Five API models from three
organizations cover the full test set (26{,}564 pairs each) ---
\textbf{Claude Haiku 4.5}, \textbf{Sonnet 4.6}, \textbf{Opus 4.7},
\textbf{Gemini 3.1 Pro}, \textbf{DeepSeek V4 Pro} (thinking
disabled) --- and \textbf{gpt-oss-20b} (a fourth organization) runs
locally on a 56/52/50-anchor sample
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results} footnotes).

Paired permutation tests on per-anchor expected Hits@1 separate
the comparisons cleanly. The L-6 beats all five API models on TIME
and EVENT at $p<10^{-4}$ ($+15$ to $+37$\,pp). The 88-anchor JURIS
split is closer: the L-6 leads Haiku, Gemini, and DeepSeek
($p\leq 0.019$), sits within noise of Sonnet ($p=0.14$), and is
indistinguishable from Opus 4.7, which holds the higher point
estimate (0.767 vs.\ 0.750, $p=0.74$). The ensemble beats Opus on
TIME/EVENT ($p<10^{-4}$) and is a coin flip on JURIS. The panel
(Figure~\ref{fig:llm_panel}) is strikingly vendor-heterogeneous:
every Claude model outscores both other API vendors' frontier
models, Haiku included, and \S\ref{sec:limitations} examines the
elicitation-format confound behind part of that spread. Under Holm
correction across the paper's $\approx$30 paired tests, every
$p<10^{-4}$ result survives; the $p\approx0.02$ JURIS-level and
on-pool-TIME margins do not, and should be read as directional.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{ssec:metrics}

We report per-anchor Hits@1 and MRR alongside pairwise AUROC, and
ship Hits@3, AUPRC, and threshold-tuned F1 with the released
metrics. Discrete scorers tie often, so Hits@k and MRR are exact
\emph{expectations over uniformly random tie-breaking}; a fixed tie
order is biased. Per-anchor metrics cover \emph{eligible} anchors: a pool of at
least two candidates with the gold present. The 37\% of
predecessors with no mined hard negative leave singleton pools and
drop out (1{,}612 of 2{,}760 EVENT-test anchors are eligible;
\S\ref{sec:benchmark}).

\label{ssec:main}

pool (\texttt{pairs.parquet}; token-overlap-mined negatives). The
best bi-encoder lifts Hits@1 5--8\,pp over zero-shot E5-small and
the cross-encoder a further 24--35\,pp. JURIS numbers ($n{=}88$)
are directional throughout.

\subsection{The construction cuts both ways: an anti-similarity baseline}
\label{ssec:anti}

\texttt{token\_set}, \texttt{tfidf\_char}, and
\texttt{sentence\_encoder} all fall below chance AUROC on
time/event (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The cause is mechanical: hard
negatives are deliberately name-similar to the predecessor while
positives are by construction name-different, so on this candidate
distribution name similarity is \emph{evidence against} a true
successor link. So we report the mirror image as its own baseline:
\texttt{anti-token\_set} ranks candidates by \emph{negated}
Jaccard, and on the curated pool it is strong
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; \emph{above} the single L-6
cross-encoder on time and event) because picking the least
name-similar candidate exploits the construction.

The supervised \texttt{features\_gbm} does the same thing, learned
rather than hand-built. Given training labels it discovers the
inversion along with the miners' residual signatures, posts the
best name-only scores in the paper (Table~\ref{tab:main_results};
above the ensemble at $p<10^{-4}$ on TIME/EVENT), and then
collapses off-pool just as hard (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}; on
JURIS, 0.36 on \textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF} and 0.12 fielded; AUROC
across splits 0.54--0.57 and 0.45--0.52).

Is that collapse a fact about feature models, or about what we fed
this one? It carries two handicaps we can remove, separately and
together: the noisy v1 negatives and the name-only feature set. On the cross-encoder's
own E5-mined negatives, the name-feature GBM improves cross-pool
but still dies fielded (0.25 / 0.25 retrieval-conditional vs.\
0.46 / 0.50); a TF-IDF-negative retrain behaves alike
(Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}, row 3). Given the full structured record (addresses,
previous names, liveness, registration status, dates, registrar;
the successor fields the labels derive from are excluded) but v1
negatives, it wins the released pool (0.87 / 0.84) yet still
fails to cross pools (0.38 / 0.35 on TF-IDF). Given
\emph{both}, it transfers across all three and wins
(\texttt{features\_gbm-full}): 0.78 / 0.75 on-pool
($+18$\,pp over the L-6, $p<10^{-4}$; $+8$\,pp over the
ensemble), 0.78 / 0.75 on TF-IDF, and 0.51--0.66 fielded
retrieval-conditional, where the enriched ensemble leads by
1--3\,pp on TIME/EVENT and trails by 6\,pp on JURIS (all n.s.)
--- at negligible inference cost. Permutation importance credits
fields no name-only method sees: candidate liveness, shared
postal/city, previous-name overlap, same registrar. Nor is the
transfer a live-vs-dead prior: ablating all four
liveness/registration-status features costs 4--7\,pp and transfer
survives (0.72 / 0.72 on TF-IDF; 0.43 / 0.47
fielded-conditional, at parity with the L-6).

Completing the grid: a cross-encoder trained with the identical
protocol but a serialized \emph{full-record} prompt (the same
fields the GBM sees) lands 2--5\,pp behind the trees on-pool
(0.76 / 0.71, $p\leq0.018$), is within 2\,pp on TF-IDF
(0.77 / 0.73), and pulls clearly ahead on the fielded
pool: 0.64 / 0.66 / 0.78 retrieval-conditional,
$+12$\,pp over the GBM and $+9$--$18$\,pp over the ensemble
($p<10^{-4}$ on TIME/EVENT); best end-to-end here (21--39\%).

The claims thus split by input class. Among name-input methods,
the L-6 is the only one whose worst pool stays strong. Given the
full record, trees and joint encoding tie on curated pools and the
cross-encoder wins the fielded universe: much of the transferable
signal is in fields no name-only method sees.

The unsupervised exploit collapses under every pool change we
test: 0.25 / 0.25 / 0.20 Hits@1 on \textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF}
(chance 0.09) against 0.53--0.66 for the cross-encoders
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), and 9--18\% fielded. It is a one-way
bet on acquirer-renaming: on TIME's M\&A slice ($n{=}1{,}506$) it
scores 0.741, above the L-6 (0.625) and the ensemble (0.711),
though not the full-record models, but only 0.11 on pure renames
and 0.35 on legal-form changes, where the cross-encoder scores 0.90
and 0.85. Curated-pool Hits@1 alone can therefore be gamed; within
the name-input class, the cross-encoder's claim is being strong
\emph{simultaneously} on all three pools and on every event type
with a usable sample. A plausible mechanism is joint attention:
asking whether the \emph{differences} between two records look like
a lifecycle-event pattern, not how similar two strings are.

\subsection{Per-event-type breakdown}
\label{ssec:per_event}

Aggregate Hits@1 hides large variation across event types.

\paragraph{Held-out time.} Pure renames
(\texttt{CHANGE\_LEGAL\_NAME}, $n{=}10$) are the easiest slice for
every similarity-direction method, zero-shot E5-small included
(9 of 10, no lift); only the inverted exploit fails them
(0.11, \S\ref{ssec:anti}). The gains come instead from events
where the post-event name is inherited from the acquirer and
string similarity cannot bridge the gap: \texttt{MERGERS\_AND\_ACQUISITIONS} ($n{=}1{,}506$: 0.18
zero-shot $\to$ 0.62 L-6 $\to$ 0.71 ensemble; the inverted
exploit's best slice, 0.74, \S\ref{ssec:anti}) and
\texttt{ABSORPTION} ($n{=}461$: 0.24 $\to$ 0.54 $\to$ 0.65). The
ensemble also more than doubles zero-shot on the small but
operationally critical \texttt{BREAKUP} subset ($n{=}7$: 0.14 $\to$
0.71).

\paragraph{``Held-out event type'' really means ``held-out
absorption.''} The held-out-event test set is 94.5\%
\texttt{ABSORPTION} anchors (\S\ref{ssec:splits});
\texttt{DEMERGER}, \texttt{BREAKUP}, and \texttt{SPINOFF}
contribute $n{=}42$, $n{=}20$, and $n{=}4$ within the Hits@1-eligible
subset. Table~\ref{tab:event_split_breakdown} shows the lift is
consistent across the absorption-like types and that the
\texttt{SPINOFF} cell is statistically uninformative, so the
aggregate ``+34\,pp Hits@1 over zero-shot E5'' on this split is
primarily a transfer claim about absorption, not a strong claim
about spinoffs.

name-related signals, so we test whether the gap survives a pool
built by a mechanically different miner.

pool's negatives are token-overlap-mined, so the headline
configuration is itself already cross-miner; the only miner-matched
cell is trn-TF-IDF on the TF-IDF pool.

\paragraph{What the 2$\times$2 tells us.} The architecture
generalizes across miners: each trained model lands within
1--2\,pp of the other on the pool it never saw (TIME/EVENT),
miner-matching is worth a bounded $+7$--$11$\,pp, and the gain
over zero-shot E5 survives every pairing ($+13$ to $+40$\,pp,
outside any CI band), though its size depends on the miner.

\paragraph{Fielded retrieve-then-rerank.} A deployment pipeline
retrieves top-K candidates from the full universe, then reranks ---
unlike the curated pools above. The trained
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} bi-encoder retrieves top-20 per test
anchor from the 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe, filtered to the
anchor's jurisdiction (the lightest production filter); the
cross-encoder reranks. The filter is nearly free (1.8--4.1\% of
positives cross countries, capping recall at $\geq$95.9\%, far
above the observed 33--50\%): the bottleneck is the embedding, not
the filter.

Bi-encoder Recall@20 is 33.2 / 39.6 / 49.7\% on TIME / EVENT /
JURIS. That is the hard ceiling for any reranker. When the bi-encoder
does retrieve the true successor, the L-6 picks it \#1 with
46--57\% Hits@1, the ensemble 53--60\%, the full-record GBM
51--66\% (n.s.\ vs.\ the ensemble) --- and the full-record
cross-encoder 64--78\%, $\geq9$\,pp clear of every other reranker
($p<10^{-4}$ on TIME/EVENT; \S\ref{ssec:anti}).
End-to-end deployment Hits@1 (recall $\times$ conditional,
un-retrieved truths counted as misses) is 15--28\% for the L-6,
17--30\% for the ensemble, 17--33\% for the full-record GBM, and
21--39\% for the full-record cross-encoder. The bottleneck is the
one the anti-correlation analysis predicts: fixed embeddings
retrieve by similarity, and lifecycle successors are often
dissimilar.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{ssec:ablations}

\paragraph{State features help modestly.} Ablating jurisdiction and
legal form from the prompt costs 1.1--1.8\,pp Hits@1 on every split,
most on held-out event type and least on held-out jurisdiction.
Scale does not substitute: moving the bi-encoder backbone from
E5-small (33M) to E5-base (110M) leaves TIME/EVENT unchanged
($-0.2 / -1.2$\,pp; JURIS $+2.3$), so on this step at least the
ceiling looks structural rather than parametric.

\paragraph{Explicit hard negatives slightly \emph{hurt} the
bi-encoder.} In Table~\ref{tab:main_results},
\texttt{method\_no\_hard} edges out the v1 full model by
0.4--1.1\,pp Hits@1 on every split, and E5-cosine re-mining does not
change that. The culprit is the
\texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} class, 92\% of mined v1
negatives and the lowest-precision one; in-batch negatives from
MultipleNegativesRanking are cleaner on their own. Better explicit
negatives, in other words, do not always help contrastive training,
even though they are exactly what the cross-encoder needs
(\S\ref{ssec:hard_negs}).

\subsection{Additional diagnostics}
\label{ssec:diagnostics}

Four diagnostics; full tables ship with the released code.
\textbf{B-cubed cluster F1}~\cite{binette2024} is 0.92 / 0.90 /
0.91 for the name+state cross-encoder and 0.94 / 0.93 / 0.94 for
the full-record one, vs.\ $\leq$0.48 for any bi-encoder
(each collapses to one giant cluster at the tuned threshold);
restricted to true clusters of size ${\geq}2$, 0.85 / 0.84 / 0.88
(name+state).
\textbf{Calibration and selective prediction.} ECE (10-bin) is
0.20 and Brier 0.18 for the name+state cross-encoder (0.13 / 0.12
full-record); temperature scaling does not fix the high-score
over-confidence, so we report raw scores. A
selective-prediction~\cite{geifman2017} sweep on the ensemble
reaches Hits@1 0.93--0.98 at 20\% coverage and 0.83--0.98 at 50\%,
so high-precision users can threshold.
\textbf{Cross-domain transfer.} On zero-shot Magellan
Walmart-Amazon~\cite{magellan2016} the cross-encoder \emph{loses}
to its own zero-shot base encoder by 5.9\,pp AUROC: the
representation is specialized to lifecycle legal-entity matching,
not generic ER.
\textbf{Label quality, second opinion.} A rule-based re-classifier,
orthogonal to the primary's event-type evidence, agrees with it on
98.7\% [95\% CI 97.0--100\%] of the 231/277 confidently-judged
positives (46 uncertain), bounding detectable label noise there at
$\approx$3\%; human review is future work.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{ssec:err}

We sampled 100 \texttt{method\_full} failures on held-out time:
66 mixed or other, 19 where anchor and true successor share
\emph{no} tokens, 8 substring-overlap distractors, and 7 exact
normalized-name collisions. The no-token cases are hardest for
name-input methods --- they need world knowledge, graph context, or
the full record (\S\ref{ssec:anti}); Figure~\ref{fig:task}'s
anchor is representative: resolving ``DB'' to Deutsche Bank from
name strings alone is beyond what a 33M encoder reliably does.

\paragraph{Residual JURIS failures cluster on sibling
consolidation.} Of the 19 / 88 anchors the ensemble mis-ranks on
JURIS, at least 11 are sibling consolidations --- the model returns
a sibling vessel instead of the parent --- plus Japanese-script
renames the ASCII tokenizer cannot bridge. Multilingual
pretraining and ownership-graph context would address both.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Headline numbers reflect the construction, not generic
lifecycle ER.} Negatives are mined name-similar and positives are
often name-different, so naive name similarity is anti-predictive on
the released pool (\S\ref{ssec:anti}). Headline numbers measure the
sub-problem this construction defines and should not be read as
generic lifecycle-ER performance. The candidate distribution matters
a great deal: scored against \emph{random} same-country pools rather
than mined ones, the marquee supplement (283 pairs, 244 anchors)
inverts the picture. Token Jaccard reaches 0.41, the name+state
cross-encoder falls below chance (0.07), and the full-record models
recover only partly (0.21--0.24). Models trained on mined pools
inherit their anti-similarity prior. Eligibility also conditions on
a name-colliding neighbour existing (\S\ref{ssec:metrics}), which
amplifies the effect.

\paragraph{Splits are pair-level, not entity-disjoint.} 32\% of
TIME-test candidate entities and 21\% of gold successors appear
somewhere in training pairs (JURIS $<$1\%). The L-6 shows no
seen-entity advantage (unseen 0.62 vs.\ seen 0.57 on TIME,
$p{=}0.04$ toward unseen; 0.57 vs.\ 0.58 on EVENT, n.s.), but an
entity-disjoint split would be a cleaner test of generalization.

\paragraph{Deployment performance is modest.} End to end, the
strongest reranker reaches only 21--39\% Hits@1, and the others
15--33\%, because first-stage Recall@20 is 33--50\%
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}). A stronger first stage (hybrid
sparse--dense retrieval, or a pre-filtered shortlist) is the
highest-leverage future work.

\paragraph{Generalization evidence is narrow.} The held-out-event
test set is 94.5\% absorption, so EVENT results show transfer to
absorption, not to spinoffs or breakups (\S\ref{ssec:per_event}).
The jurisdiction test has 88 eligible anchors ($\pm$8.5\,pp CI), so
JURIS results are directional. GLEIF \texttt{SuccessorEntity} is
filed voluntarily, which biases positives toward well-counseled
regulated entities; 283 of 302 marquee Wikidata M\&A pairs are
absent from it. A second-classifier check bounds detectable label
noise at $\approx$3\% (\S\ref{ssec:diagnostics}); human review is
future work.

\paragraph{LLM baselines bound one elicitation format.} On
TIME/EVENT the 0--10 rubric costs the five API models 13--48\,pp
Hits@1 in within-pool ties: Gemini 3.1 Pro scores every candidate
identically on 55\% of TIME anchors, and oracle tie-breaking would
lift it from 0.23 to 0.72, whereas Sonnet and Opus tie only
$\approx$5\% of pools. Batched Sonnet 4.6 also beats single-pair
on the same 56 TIME anchors (0.46 vs.\ 0.39). The panel bounds what
this format extracts, not what these models can do; finer scales,
log-probs, or a full-record prompt would likely raise it, and
cross-vendor gaps may partly reflect exposure to GLEIF-derived text.

\paragraph{Smaller caveats.} Over three seeds the bi-encoder and
enriched cross-encoder vary $\leq$1.6\,pp and the full-record models
$\leq$3.4\,pp on-pool; fielded, the full-record cross-encoder's
worst seed still leads the GBM's best by 8\,pp on TIME/EVENT. Of
the hard negatives, 0.22\% appear in GLEIF's
\texttt{SuccessorEntity}; dropping them moves results
$\leq$0.2\,pp. Parent/subsidiary negatives regress under prompt
enrichment (\S\ref{ssec:negclass}); at 99 examples the effect is
weak, but related-but-distinct entities merit explicit modeling.

\section{Conclusion}

\textsc{Sylvester} is an open benchmark for lifecycle-aware entity
resolution on financial entities, built from public GLEIF, SEC
EDGAR, and Wikidata data, with a three-pool protocol that separates
methods that learn the pool from methods that learn the task.
Name-only exploits post the best name-input scores and then
collapse off-pool; among name-input methods, a 22M-parameter
cross-encoder survives and stays ahead of every zero-shot LLM we
test on the two large splits. Input matters as much as
architecture: boosted trees over the full registry record transfer
across all three pools, and a cross-encoder over the same record
wins the fielded universe by 12\,pp. The evidence is strongest for
held-out time; event-type transfer is mostly transfer to
absorption, and jurisdiction results are directional. What remains
open is the first stage: with 33--50\% retrieval recall, it is
candidate generation, not matching, where lifecycle ER breaks.

parsers, predictions, trained weights, and a datasheet are at

reproduces from raw GLEIF in $\approx$2 hours on one MacBook.
