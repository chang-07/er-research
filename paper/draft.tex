\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textsc{TemporalLink}, an open benchmark for
\emph{lifecycle-aware} entity resolution on financial entities:
matching records across mergers, rebrands, spinoffs, and
jurisdiction migrations, where the surface form has deliberately
changed. Built from public GLEIF Level-1/2, SEC EDGAR, and
Wikidata, it provides 125{,}519 labelled pairs over 3.3M entities
and three held-out evaluation regimes (time, event type, jurisdiction).

The benchmark's construction shapes what the numbers mean.
Hard negatives are deliberately mined to be name-similar to each
predecessor and true successors are by construction name-different,
so on this candidate distribution similarity-based baselines (token,
char-TF-IDF, zero-shot E5) sit below 0.5 AUROC on the time and event
splits --- a property of how we picked negatives, not a claim about
name similarity in ER generally. A fine-tuned 22M-parameter MiniLM
cross-encoder reaches 60.5 / 56.5 / 75.0\% Hits@1
($\pm$2.2 / 2.4 / 8.8\,pp 95\% bootstrap CI) and AUROC 0.83--0.87
on the three splits, 24--35\,pp Hits@1 over the strongest
bi-encoder. The per-event-type breakdown is the most honest view:
the lift concentrates on M\&A, absorption, and breakup
($+30$ to $+45$\,pp), while pure renames are already at 0.90
Hits@1 zero-shot.

Three caveats qualify the headline. Re-mining negatives with
TF-IDF char-3gram cosine shrinks the cross-encoder advantage
5--15\,pp but keeps it $+13$ to $+38$\,pp above zero-shot. A
fielded retrieve-then-rerank simulation over the full 3.3M-entity
universe drops end-to-end Hits@1 to 15--30\,\%: the bi-encoder's
Recall@20 of 33--50\,\% is the upstream bottleneck. Against a
Sonnet-4.6 LLM baseline the cross-encoder gap is decisive only
on the absorption-heavy EVENT split ($p = 0.004$); on TIME and
JURIS we cannot distinguish the two. Code, splits,
predictions, and trained weights reproduce on a single MacBook
in $\sim$2 hours.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Twitter Inc.\ became X Corp.\ Facebook became Meta. Sprint was absorbed into
T-Mobile. Diamond Bank was acquired by Access Bank. Every fintech engineer,
every quant researcher, and every compliance analyst has a personal story of
their pipeline silently breaking on one of these transitions. The records
look completely different on the surface --- different legal names, different
tickers, sometimes different jurisdictions and legal forms --- yet they
describe the same continuing economic entity.

Pair-matching ER --- including LLM-based methods that achieve 99\%
F1 on point-in-time records~\cite{opensanctions2026} --- treats each
record as a snapshot of identity. Temporal entity-linking benchmarks
such as TempEL~\cite{tempel2022} address mention-to-KB disambiguation
against an evolving knowledge base, a different task. Financial KG
construction~\cite{fingraphex2025,finreflectkg2025} works at a single
time slice. To the best of our knowledge no public benchmark targets
the specific question we care about: \emph{given two records observed
at different times, do they describe the same economic entity,
accounting for any lifecycle event that may have occurred between
them?}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An open benchmark for lifecycle-aware financial ER}
        (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}): 125{,}519 labelled pairs over
        3.3M GLEIF Level-1 entities, hard negatives in four classes
        backed by 473K GLEIF Level-2 relationships, three held-out
        evaluation regimes (time, event type, jurisdiction). Every
        construction choice and what it does and doesn't generalise
        to is documented (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}, \S\ref{sec:limitations}).

  \item \textbf{An empirical characterisation} (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}):
        three similarity baselines (token, char-TF-IDF, zero-shot E5)
        fall below 0.5 AUROC on the time and event splits --- a direct
        consequence of mining same-name negatives against name-different
        positives, \emph{not} a universal claim about name similarity in
        ER. A 22M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder fine-tuned on benchmark
        positives and mined hard negatives reaches 60.5 / 56.5 / 75.0\%
        Hits@1 ($\pm$2.2 / 2.4 / 8.8\,pp) and AUROC 0.83--0.87 across
        the three splits, 24--35\,pp Hits@1 over the strongest
        bi-encoder. Per-event analysis (\S\ref{ssec:per_event})
        localises the lift to M\&A, absorption, and breakup events,
        where the post-event name comes from the acquirer; pure
        renames are already at 0.90 Hits@1 zero-shot.

  \item \textbf{Robustness checks across two candidate-pool shifts}
        (Section~\ref{ssec:robustness}). Re-mining hard negatives
        with TF-IDF char-3gram cosine and re-evaluating the
        $2{\times}2$ train/test matrix shrinks the cross-encoder
        advantage 5--15\,pp but keeps it $+13$ to $+38$\,pp above
        zero-shot on every off-diagonal cell. A fielded retrieve-
        then-rerank simulation --- bi-encoder retrieves top-20
        candidates per anchor from the full 3.3M-entity universe,
        cross-encoder reranks --- drops end-to-end Hits@1 to
        15--30\,\%: the upstream Recall@20 of 33--50\,\% caps
        everything. Reranker conditional Hits@1 (given truth in
        top-20) is 46--60\,\%; the gap between the single L-6
        cross-encoder and an L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble is small once
        retrieval is the bottleneck.
\end{enumerate}

The full pipeline reproduces on a single MacBook in $\sim$2 hours
--- no GPU, no proprietary data, no paid annotation. A
prompt-enrichment-plus-ensemble extension that lifts Hits@1 a
further $+9$ to $+11$\,pp on time/event ($+3$ on jurisdiction, inside
its CI band; Section~\ref{ssec:main}) is described briefly in the
experiments section; the headline result above is the single
fine-tuned cross-encoder.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Entity resolution and record linkage.} Classical
ER~\cite{fellegisunter1969} matches records via similarity over
structured fields. Modern PLM cross-encoders on serialized records
(GraLMatch~\cite{gralmatch2025}) are now standard, and the
OpenSanctions Pairs corpus~\cite{opensanctions2026} benchmarks LLM
matching at 755K pairs. All adopt a \emph{snapshot} framing, not
lifecycle-spanning matches.

\paragraph{Temporal entity linking.} TempEL~\cite{tempel2022}
time-stratifies entity linking against an evolving Wikipedia, but
the task is mention-to-KB disambiguation rather than record-to-record
matching, and the entities are general-purpose rather than financial
legal entities with structured lifecycle events.

\paragraph{Financial entity knowledge graphs.} Recent work at the ACM
Conference on AI in Finance constructs financial-filing knowledge
graphs (FinGraphEx~\cite{fingraphex2025},
FinReflectKG~\cite{finreflectkg2025}) at a single time slice; the
lifecycle-bridge question is orthogonal.

\section{The TemporalLink Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Data sources}

We construct \textsc{TemporalLink} entirely from open, persistently-licensed
sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-1 LEI-CDF concatenated file.} The Global Legal
        Entity Identifier Foundation publishes the LEI repository (3.3M
        legal entities as of our 2026-05-19 snapshot) every eight hours,
        free of charge, under CC0. Each \texttt{LEIRecord} includes the
        entity's legal name, jurisdiction, legal form, registered and
        headquarters addresses, registration metadata, and crucially the
        \texttt{LegalEntityEvents} container --- a structured log of
        completed and in-progress lifecycle events with effective and
        recorded dates --- and a \texttt{SuccessorEntity} field that
        explicitly links predecessor to successor at the LEI level.

  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-2 Relationship Records.} 473{,}839 typed
        relationships between LEIs (direct/ultimate consolidation, fund
        management, sub-fund, international branch), used in our
        hard-negative construction.

  \item \textbf{Wikidata.} High-profile mergers and acquisitions captured
        via SPARQL queries on the P156 (\emph{followed by}) property,
        restricted to instances of business / organization. Used as a
        marquee qualitative supplement and for cross-validation.

  \item \textbf{SEC EDGAR.} The 10{,}354-record
        \texttt{company\_tickers.json} file gives a free name $\leftrightarrow$
        CIK crosswalk that maps to GLEIF for the U.S.\ public-company
        subset (3{,}334 confident name-match links). EDGAR 8-K Item 5.03
        filings (corporate name changes) are deferred to v2 as a US-
        specific supplementary positive source.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positive labels}

Our gold-standard positives are GLEIF \texttt{SuccessorEntity} links: explicit
predecessor-LEI $\to$ successor-LEI pairs maintained by GLEIF's network of
Local Operating Units. The 2026-05-19 snapshot contains 50{,}202 records
with a non-null \texttt{SuccessorEntity} element; of these, 33{,}645 have a
populated \texttt{SuccessorLEI} (the remaining 16{,}557 reference a
successor entity that never registered an LEI --- useful for entity-death
modeling but excluded from v1 pair-wise training).

\paragraph{Label quality validation.} A five-class auto-classifier on
the successor pairs (using documented event types, normalized name
delta, jurisdiction, legal form) yields: \texttt{strong\_real}
(documented event + name change) 25{,}428 (75.6\%);
\texttt{likely\_real\_name\_changed} 3{,}330 (9.9\%);
\texttt{likely\_real\_event\_only} 1{,}704 (5.1\%); \texttt{ambiguous}
1{,}355 (4.0\%); \textbf{\texttt{likely\_administrative}} 1{,}828
(5.4\%, filtered out). We retain the first four classes (94.6\%),
yielding 31{,}824 plausibly-real lifecycle-spanning pairs.

\subsection{Hard negatives}

For each positive predecessor we mine up to 80 hard negatives drawn from
four classes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (221 pairs) --- LEIs that share a
        Level-2 RR edge (consolidation, fund-management, sub-fund) with the
        predecessor. These look related but are legally distinct.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} (10{,}208) --- distinct LEIs with
        identical normalized legal names in the same jurisdiction. The
        purest test of ``can the model distinguish coincidental name
        collision from true lifecycle linkage.''
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (10{,}703) --- distinct LEIs
        with identical normalized name in a different country.
  \item \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (232{,}601) --- fuzzy
        fallback: distinct LEIs in the same country sharing $\geq 2$
        content tokens after normalization, ranked by Jaccard. Covers the
        long tail when name-exact collisions are sparse.
\end{itemize}

After deduplication: 253{,}733 candidate (predecessor, hard-negative) pairs
covering 20{,}138 of 31{,}817 predecessors ($\sim$63\%). The remaining 37\%
are small entities in jurisdictions without name collisions; they fall back
to random in-batch negatives during training.

\subsection{Evaluation splits}
\label{ssec:splits}

For each positive we identify its \emph{anchor lifecycle event} --- the
highest-priority COMPLETED event on the predecessor's record by type-
priority then effective date. We then assign each positive (and its
associated negatives) to one of three split regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-out time:} anchor effective date $<$ 2025-01-01
        (train) vs.\ $\geq$ 2025-01-01 (test); Figure~\ref{fig:histogram}
        shows the underlying event-volume distribution. Test composition:
        3{,}448 positives, $\sim$2{,}000+ anchors with at least one positive.
  \item \textbf{Held-out event type:} train on M\&A,
        rename, legal-form change; test on spinoff, demerger,
        breakup, absorption. The test set is heavily dominated by
        \emph{absorption} (2{,}609 of 2{,}760 anchors, 94.5\%);
        demerger (120), breakup (25), and spinoff (6) are present but
        statistically thin. We report per-event-type metrics within
        the split (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) so each held-out
        type's contribution is visible.
  \item \textbf{Held-out jurisdiction:} train on US + GB + DE; test on
        SG + JP + BR. Test composition: 88 anchors, 161 positives ---
        the smallest of the three splits. Headline numbers on this
        split carry $\sim$$\pm$8.8\,pp 95\% bootstrap CIs (vs.\
        $\sim$$\pm$1.7\,pp on time/event); see Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.
\end{itemize}

Each positive gets up to 5 hard negatives if its splits are all
\texttt{train}, or up to 10 if any split places it in \texttt{test}, yielding
125{,}519 final pair rows.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We present two methods at different cost-accuracy points. The bi-encoder
(Section~\ref{ssec:bi}) embeds each side independently and scores by cosine
--- cheap, single-pass, but hemmed in by the anti-correlation property of
name embeddings on this task. The cross-encoder (Section~\ref{ssec:ce})
reads both records together --- 4--10$\times$ more expensive per pair, but
no longer asked to keep name-different records cosine-close. It produces
the headline result.

\subsection{Bi-encoder}
\label{ssec:bi}

We fine-tune \texttt{intfloat/e5-small-v2} (33M params, 384-dim) via
supervised contrastive learning with state features folded into the
text prompt: \texttt{"query: } $n_\ell$ \texttt{| jurisdiction: }
$c_\ell$ \texttt{| form: } $f_\ell$\texttt{"}. Training uses the
26{,}297 positives in the strict no-leakage intersection of all
three split regimes (one hard neg per anchor), MultipleNegativesRanking
loss (InfoNCE; in-batch + explicit hard neg as negatives), 3 epochs,
batch 32, AdamW lr$=2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup, $\sim$6 min on MPS.
Ablation variants: \texttt{method\_no\_state} (name only),
\texttt{method\_no\_hard} (in-batch negs only), \texttt{method\_v2\_full}
(adds v2 E5-cosine negs + 1{,}372 transitive positives). Loss is
contrastive; the cross-encoder below uses BCE on the logit --- different
objectives by architectural convention, not strictly controlled.

\subsection{Cross-encoder}
\label{ssec:ce}

A bi-encoder has to place each record at a fixed point in
embedding space. That is a hard ask on lifecycle ER:
the event generating each positive deliberately changes the record's
surface form, so the bi-encoder is being asked to keep cosine-close two
records that, on the page, look very different. A cross-encoder gets to read both
records jointly; it can compare specific tokens between them rather
than each side in isolation.

We fine-tune a second-stage cross-encoder that ingests
$(\text{record}_A, \text{record}_B)$ as a single concatenated sequence
and emits a single relevance logit. The backbone is the
22M-parameter \texttt{ms-marco-\allowbreak MiniLM-L-6-v2} cross-encoder
from HuggingFace; training uses the same positive set as the
bi-encoder (including transitives) plus a 4:1 sample of v2 hard
negatives per positive, with positives labelled $1.0$ and negatives
$0.0$.
Loss: standard binary cross-entropy on the logit. 2 epochs, batch 32, lr
$2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup steps. v4--v10 extensions
(\S\ref{ssec:main}) hold architecture/optimizer constant and modify only
the serialized text, the training positives, and the backbone.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} Scoring all 125{,}519 pairs in the benchmark
takes $\approx$80 seconds on MPS. In a deployed retrieval pipeline the
cross-encoder would re-rank a bi-encoder's top-$K$ candidates rather than
score every pair --- the standard two-stage pattern in information retrieval.
Our benchmark's hard-negative pool is fixed (5--10 negs per positive), so
direct full scoring is feasible and the numbers in
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reflect that setting.

\paragraph{v2 hard-negative re-mining.}
\label{ssec:hard_negs}
The v1 token-overlap fallback (92\% of negs) injected enough label
noise that explicit hard-negs slightly underperformed in-batch alone.
v2 replaces it with E5-cosine top-20 same-country candidates,
yielding 652{,}764 total negs across 31{,}714 predecessors (vs 253{,}733
v1; coverage $\sim$20K $\to$ 31{,}714). v2 hard negs erase the
``hard-negs hurt'' anomaly for the bi-encoder and noticeably help
the cross-encoder.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four similarity baselines:
\texttt{exact\_name} (normalized legal-name equality),
\texttt{token\_set} (Jaccard over normalized name tokens),
\texttt{tfidf\_char} (char-3gram TF-IDF cosine fit on the union of
legal names in the benchmark), and \texttt{sentence\_encoder}
(zero-shot E5-small-v2 cosine on the legal name --- same backbone
as the method, without fine-tuning). We also report three
zero-shot LLM pairwise baselines on a stratified test-set sample
(56 / 52 / 50 anchors for TIME / EVENT / JURIS respectively, with
all candidate pairs per anchor scored): Anthropic's
\textbf{Claude Haiku 4.5} via API ($\approx$\$1.50 spend with
prompt-caching), Anthropic's \textbf{Claude Sonnet 4.6} run through
Claude Code subagents in 25\,s wall-clock with 16-way parallelism
($\approx$444K plan tokens), and OpenAI's \textbf{gpt-oss-20b}
run locally on
Apple Silicon via Ollama. Each pair is scored 0--10 by the model
(a numeric score is more usable than a binary YES/NO since it
breaks ranking ties). Per-split Hits@1 + MRR for all three appear
in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.

Paired McNemar tests on per-anchor Hits@1 (shared anchors)
separate the comparisons cleanly. Versus Haiku and gpt-oss-20b,
the cross-encoder gap clears noise on every split (Haiku
$p < 10^{-4}$ on TIME/EVENT, $p = 3\!\times\!10^{-4}$ on JURIS,
discordant $\geq 4.5\!:\!1$; $16/16$ JURIS discordant pairs go to
v10 over gpt-oss). Sonnet 4.6 is tighter: v2 wins EVENT decisively
($+0.31$, $p = 0.004$) but TIME ($+0.11$, $p = 0.26$) and JURIS
($+0.08$, $p = 0.45$) are within sample noise. The L-6$\times$L-12
ensemble clears noise on TIME ($+0.25$, $p = 0.004$) and EVENT
($+0.35$, $p < 10^{-3}$) but not JURIS ($+0.14$, $p = 0.12$). The
cross-encoder dominates the absorption-heavy split where lifecycle
reasoning is hardest; on the 50-anchor JURIS sample we cannot
distinguish it from a frontier LLM.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report per-anchor retrieval metrics (Hits@1, Hits@3, MRR) computed by
ranking each anchor's candidates by score; AUROC and AUPRC for pair-wise
classification framing; and the train-tuned F1 at the F1-optimal threshold.

\paragraph{Extensions: enriched prompt + L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble.}
Stacking four modifications on the headline cross-encoder ---
prompt enrichment (GLEIF Level-2 parent/child names, founding year,
ISO country expansion, category), Wikidata-LEI mining (259 GLEIF
positives recovered, 49 cross-jurisdiction), multi-seed averaging,
and ensembling with an L-12-backbone variant --- lifts Hits@1 a
further $+9$ to $+11$\,pp on time/event ($+3$ on jurisdiction,
inside its CI band; Table~\ref{tab:v7}, Figure~\ref{fig:cum_gains}).
The ensemble reaches 0.700 / 0.677 / 0.784 Hits@1; the headline
result reported against baselines remains the single L-6
cross-encoder, so that comparison stays apples-to-apples. A negative
result is worth recording: symmetric scoring (averaging $s(a,b)$ and
$s(b,a)$) hurts $-0.9 / -1.9 / -9.1$\,pp, so the cross-encoder really
does learn a directional predecessor$\to$successor representation.

\paragraph{Backbone size does not break the bi-encoder ceiling.}
Scaling E5-small (33M) to E5-base (110M) leaves TIME/EVENT Hits@1
unchanged ($-0.2 / -1.2$\,pp) and lifts JURIS $+2.3$\,pp. The
bi-encoder's constraint is structural, not parameter-bound: cosine-
close embeddings for lexically-different lifecycle pairs are not a
problem more parameters fix.


\subsection{Why similarity baselines score below 0.5 AUROC}

\texttt{token\_set}, \texttt{tfidf\_char}, and
\texttt{sentence\_encoder} all land below 0.5 AUROC on the
held-out-time and held-out-event-type splits
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The cause is mechanical: hard
negatives are deliberately name-similar to the predecessor while
positives are by construction name-different, so on this candidate
distribution name similarity is \emph{evidence against} a true
successor link. The same baselines evaluated against random
negatives would behave as expected. The finding motivates joint
encoding here; we do not extend it to ER in general.

Fine-tuning a bi-encoder helps only marginally: AUROC rises from
0.32 to $\sim$0.41 on time and 0.39 to $\sim$0.46 on event, still
below 0.5; Hits@1
lifts 5--8\,pp over zero-shot but the model still has to place
each record at a single point in embedding space. The
cross-encoder breaks past that ceiling: 0.83--0.87 AUROC across
all three splits, where every non-cross-encoder method stays at
or below 0.55 on TIME and EVENT. The
mechanism is joint attention across both records: the model can
ask whether the differences between them look like a known
lifecycle-event pattern, not just whether two strings are similar.

\subsection{Where the lift is real: per-event-type breakdown}
\label{ssec:per_event}

Aggregate Hits@1 hides large per-event-type variation. The
cross-encoder does real work on M\&A, absorption, and breakup
events, where the post-event name comes from the acquirer rather
than the target and string-similarity cannot bridge the gap. On
pure renames it does essentially nothing --- those are already at
0.90 Hits@1 zero-shot, since the name change tends to be small
and similarity carries the day.

\paragraph{On the held-out-time split (Figure~\ref{fig:per_event}),
the cross-encoder's gain is concentrated in the operationally hard
events.} Pure renames (\texttt{CHANGE\_LEGAL\_NAME}, $n=10$) are
essentially solved by every method including zero-shot E5-small
(0.90 Hits@1, no lift). The headline gains come from
\texttt{MERGERS\_AND\_ACQUISITIONS} ($n=1{,}506$: 0.18 zero-shot
$\to$ 0.62 v2 $\to$ 0.71 v10) and \texttt{ABSORPTION} ($n=461$:
0.23 $\to$ 0.53 $\to$ 0.65), the cases where the post-event name
comes from the acquirer rather than the target and pure name
similarity has nothing to grip onto. v10 also more
than doubles zero-shot on the small but operationally critical
\texttt{BREAKUP} subset ($n=7$: 0.14 $\to$ 0.71).

\paragraph{On the held-out-event split, ``held-out event type'' really
means ``held-out absorption.''} The held-out-event test set is 94.5\%
\texttt{ABSORPTION} anchors (Section~\ref{ssec:splits}); within the
Hits@1-eligible subset, \texttt{DEMERGER}, \texttt{BREAKUP}, and
\texttt{SPINOFF} contribute $n=42$, $n=20$, and $n=4$ respectively. We report
Table~\ref{tab:event_split_breakdown} so that the contribution of each
held-out type is visible separately. The cross-encoder lift is
consistent across the absorption-like types (\texttt{ABSORPTION}: 0.22
$\to$ 0.57, \texttt{DEMERGER}: 0.29 $\to$ 0.55, \texttt{BREAKUP}: 0.15
$\to$ 0.40); the \texttt{SPINOFF} cell is statistically meaningless at
$n=4$. The aggregate ``+34 pp Hits@1 on the event split'' number is
therefore primarily a transfer claim about absorption, not a strong
claim about spinoffs.

\subsection{Robustness to candidate-pool shift}
\label{ssec:robustness}

The headline cross-encoder result is trained and (mostly) evaluated against
candidates from the same family of hard-negative miners --- v1
token-overlap and v2 E5-cosine both rank candidates by name-related signals,
and the cross-encoder learns on v2 negs. The obvious question: does the gap
survive an evaluation pool built with a mechanically different miner?  If
yes, the cross-encoder advantage is a property of the method; if no, the
headline number is partly an artefact of how the negatives were chosen.

\paragraph{Setup.} We re-mine hard negatives with sparse char-3gram
TF-IDF cosine (same signal as the \texttt{tfidf\_char} baseline) ---
chosen because it has no learned representation and shares no
parameters with E5. The other three classes (parent/subsidiary,
same-name same/diff-country) are held constant. We call the resulting
pair set \textsc{TemporalLink-v3}; splits and positives are unchanged.
We train two cross-encoders (one on v2 hard negs, one on v3) and score
each on both pair sets. The $2\times 2$ train/evaluate matrix has
diagonal cells in-distribution, off-diagonal cells out-of-distribution
at the candidate-pool level.

\paragraph{What the 2$\times$2 tells us.} First, the cross-encoder \emph{architecture}
generalizes well across miners: the v3-trained cross-encoder evaluated on
the v2 pool reaches 0.588 / 0.533 / 0.750 Hits@1 --- within 1--4\,pp of
the v2-trained model on the same pool, despite never having seen E5-mined
candidates during training. The result is symmetric: the v2-trained model
on the v3 pool likewise stays well above any non-cross-encoder method.

Second, there is a real but bounded in-distribution effect. The v2-trained
cross-encoder loses 5--13\,pp Hits@1 when moved to the v3 evaluation pool
(0.600 $\to$ 0.545 on time; 0.705 $\to$ 0.571 on jurisdiction). Conversely,
the v3-trained model gains $+7$\,pp on v3 over the v2-trained one. So
training on the same miner used to construct the eval candidates is worth
roughly 5--15\,pp Hits@1 --- non-trivial, but not the entire headline gap.

Third, the cross-encoder gain over zero-shot E5 \emph{is robust} to the
miner if we are willing to retrain the cross-encoder: the diagonal cells
(in-distribution) show $+20$ to $+40$\,pp gains across all six splits.
The off-diagonal cells (out-of-distribution at training time) show
$+13$ to $+38$\,pp gains --- smaller, but still well outside any plausible
CI band. Joint encoding does real work on lifecycle ER --- but the absolute
size of the gain depends on how the candidate pool was mined, so the
``$+24$--$35$\,pp'' figure in the abstract should be read in that light.

\paragraph{Fielded retrieve-then-rerank.} The miner-robustness
experiment above shifts the mining signal but still draws candidates
from a curated negative pool. A real deployment pipeline does
something different: a first-stage retriever pulls the top-K
candidates from the full entity universe, then a second-stage model
reranks. We simulate that here. The trained
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} bi-encoder retrieves top-20 candidates per
test anchor from the full 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe, restricted to
the anchor's jurisdiction (the lightest filter a production system
would apply); the cross-encoder reranks.

Bi-encoder Recall@20 is 33.2 / 39.6 / 49.7\% on TIME / EVENT /
JURIS. That is the hard ceiling for any reranker. When the bi-encoder
does retrieve the true successor, the v2 cross-encoder picks it
\#1 with 46--58\% Hits@1 and the v10 ensemble reaches 53--60\% ---
both rerankers re-score the actual fielded candidates, and the v10
gain over v2 is modest once the bi-encoder distractors look
genuinely lifecycle-plausible. End-to-end deployment Hits@1
(recall $\times$ conditional, un-retrieved truths counted as
misses) is 15--29\% for v2 and 17--30\% for v10. The drop from
56--75\% in-distribution to 15--30\% deployment confirms rather
than contradicts \S\ref{ssec:bi}: the bi-encoder's fixed-embedding
limit, which fails to retrieve lifecycle-spanning successors by
cosine similarity, is exactly the bottleneck the anti-correlation
analysis predicts.

\subsection{Ablations}

Both ablations are in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.

\textbf{State features help modestly.} Removing jurisdiction and
legal form from the prompt (\texttt{method\_no\_state}) costs
1.5--2\,pp Hits@1 vs.\ \texttt{method\_full} on every split. The
biggest contribution is on held-out-jurisdiction (where the
country token does most of the work) and the smallest is on
held-out-event-type (where the name change itself does it).

\textbf{Explicit hard negatives slightly \emph{hurt} in v1.}
\texttt{method\_no\_hard} edges out \texttt{method\_full} by 0.4--1.1\,pp
Hits@1 on every split. The culprit is the
\texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} class, 92\% of all mined v1
hard negatives and the lowest-precision class --- the fuzzy
fallback injects label noise, and the in-batch negatives from
MultipleNegativesRanking are cleaner on their own. This motivates
the v2 mining switch from token-overlap to E5-cosine, and pushes
back on the default assumption that more hard negatives is always
better.

\subsection{Additional diagnostics}
\label{ssec:diagnostics}

Four diagnostics, compressed to fit the page budget; full tables
and figures are in the released code.
\textbf{B-cubed cluster F1}~\cite{binette2024} on the test
partition is 0.92 / 0.90 / 0.91 for the cross-encoder vs.\
$\leq$0.47 for any bi-encoder (each of which collapses to one
giant cluster at the tuned threshold). Restricting to records in
true clusters of size $\geq 2$ --- 64\% of test records are
singletons --- gives a more honest 0.85 / 0.84 / 0.88.
\textbf{Calibration and selective prediction.} ECE (10-bin) is
0.20, Brier 0.18: the model is over-confident in its high-score
bin, consistent with recent PLM-EM calibration
results~\cite{llmemcalib2025}. A
selective-prediction~\cite{geifman2017} sweep on v10 hits Hits@1
$\geq 0.94$ at 20\% coverage and 0.87--0.97 at 50\% across the
three splits; users who need high precision can just threshold on
the score (curve in repo).
\textbf{Cross-domain transfer.} Zero-shot Magellan Walmart-Amazon:
the cross-encoder \emph{loses} to its own zero-shot base encoder
by 5.9\,pp AUROC. The fine-tuned representation is specialised to
lifecycle-spanning legal-entity matching, not generic ER.
\textbf{Secondary label-quality check.} 277 sampled positives
re-classified by an orthogonal heuristic agree with the primary
GLEIF auto-classifier on 98.7\% [95\% CI 97.0--100\%] of the
confident (231/277) subset, bounding label noise below 2\% there;
the other 16.6\% were marked uncertain.

\subsection{Where each method is fooled}

The negative-class breakdown (Table~\ref{tab:neg_class}) reports per-
negative-class mean score: higher means the method is more often fooled
by that class of negative. The bi-encoder fine-tunes roughly halve the
zero-shot E5 baseline's score on \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} and
\texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} negatives, but \texttt{same\_name\_
same\_country} remains a wall at $\approx$0.90 mean score: the bi-encoder
cannot reliably tell a real successor link from two distinct entities
sharing an exact normalized name in the same country.

The cross-encoder breaks that wall: \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country}
mean score drops from 0.90 (best bi-encoder) to 0.45, and the v10
ensemble drops it further to 0.34. v10 also nearly solves
\texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (0.92 zero-shot $\to$ 0.16) and
\texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (0.91 $\to$ 0.25). The one
regression is \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (0.45 bi-encoder $\to$ 0.70
v2 cross-encoder $\to$ 0.78 v10): the joint encoder occasionally
treats parent--subsidiary edges as positive lifecycle bridges, and the
v10 prompt enrichment that injects GLEIF L2 parent/child names makes
the regression \emph{worse}, not better. With only 221 examples in
this class the headline impact is small, but the trend points at a
clean v3 target: explicit multi-class modeling of corporate-hierarchy
relationships.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Error analysis}

We sampled 100 \texttt{method\_full} failures on the held-out-time test split
(positives that were not ranked first against their hard-negative set) and
categorized them via simple text features. The categories:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Category & Count \\
\midrule
other (mixed) & 66 \\
anchor and true successor share \emph{no} tokens & 19 \\
substring name overlap with a wrong candidate & 8 \\
exact normalized-name collision with a wrong candidate & 7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The 19 ``no shared tokens'' cases are the hardest: a name like
\texttt{Nima N.V.} (NL) whose true successor is
\texttt{Beheermaatschappij Arros B.V.}\ shares zero meaningful tokens with
either the predecessor or the wrong top-1 candidate \texttt{NIMA B.V.}. No
similarity-based representation can solve these; they require world
knowledge or explicit graph context (board overlap, regulatory filings,
press releases). One representative failure: anchor
\texttt{DB HR Solutions GmbH} (DE), true successor
\texttt{DEUTSCHE BANK AKTIENGESELLSCHAFT}, top-1 wrong
\texttt{HR Solutions GmbH} (a token-overlap negative). The DB$\to$Deutsche
Bank disambiguation requires recognizing ``DB'' as a known abbreviation
for ``Deutsche Bank'' --- an external knowledge fact a 33M-parameter
encoder is unlikely to memorize from name strings alone.

\paragraph{v10 residual JURIS failures cluster on sibling
consolidation.} Of the 19 / 88 anchors v10 mis-ranks on JURIS,
$\geq$11 are sibling consolidations (e.g., 9 ``\texttt{EPIC <port>
PTE.\ LTD.}'' Singapore ships into \texttt{BW EPIC KOSAN VESSELS
ONE}; the model returns sibling \texttt{EPIC} vessels); a second
cluster is Japanese-script name changes the ASCII tokenizer cannot
bridge (Daiwa-Sumigin $\to$ Mitsui-Sumitomo DS). An LLM with
multilingual pretraining and ownership-graph context would
address both.

\subsection{Why this matters in practice}

The benchmark targets a step that every fintech infrastructure company we
know runs: Stripe trains merchant
embeddings~\cite{stripe_ai} and fights AI-driven rebranding
fraud~\cite{stripe_lp_fraud}; Ramp performs
vendor deduplication across $\sim$27M spreadsheet cells in its
agentic-accounting product~\cite{finch2025}; Bloomberg's entity
backbone underpins both its NLP work and the Quant Research
Delphyne foundation model~\cite{bloomberg_delphyne2025}. Each team
reports lifecycle-ER failures as recurring operational pain points;
retail brokerages run analogous corporate-action reconciliation across
pre- and post-event references. \textsc{TemporalLink} gives them a
public benchmark to evaluate against.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Six limitations; the released code carries the longer versions
(cost/latency, calibration curves, error taxonomy, label-quality).

\paragraph{The ``anti-correlation'' finding is a construction artefact.}
Hard negatives in \textsc{TemporalLink} are mined to be
name-similar to the predecessor; positives are by definition
name-different. The sub-0.5 AUROC of similarity baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) follows directly from that mining
design and does not generalise to ER outside this benchmark. The
finding motivates joint encoding here; it is not a universal claim
about name similarity in ER.

\paragraph{In-distribution headline overstates deployment.}
The headline cross-encoder Hits@1 (56--75\,\%) is on the v2 mined
candidate pool; on a fielded retrieve-then-rerank pipeline that
draws candidates from the full 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}), end-to-end Hits@1 falls to 15--29\,\%
(v2) / 17--30\,\% (v10). The cause is upstream: bi-encoder
Recall@20 is 33--50\,\%, so the reranker often never sees the true
successor. The miner-robustness experiment shows the cross-encoder
gain itself is partly in-distribution but not purely a within-miner
artefact ($+13$ to $+38$\,pp off-diagonal). A stronger first-stage
retriever --- a learned hybrid sparse-dense index, or a coarsely
pre-filtered cross-encoder shortlist --- is the highest-leverage
v3 target.

\paragraph{Seed variance and a sliver of mislabeled hard negatives.}
Bi-encoder Hits@1 over seeds 42/7/1337 was $0.251 \pm 0.005$
(TIME), $0.291 \pm 0.004$ (EVENT), $0.523 \pm 0.016$ (JURIS);
TIME/EVENT are stable, JURIS varies $\pm$1.6\,pp consistent with
its small test sample ($n = 88$). Cross-encoder multi-seed
retraining (v4 ensemble, Table~\ref{tab:v7}) shows similar
behaviour. Separately, $205/93{,}695 = 0.22\%$ of hard negatives
appear in GLEIF's \texttt{SuccessorEntity}; the cleaned variant
\texttt{pairs\_clean.parquet} drops them with $\leq$0.2\,pp impact.

\paragraph{Reporting / survivorship bias + thin slices.} GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} is voluntarily populated, biasing positives
toward well-counseled regulated entities. The held-out-event test set
is 94.5\% absorption (the label oversells event-type generality), and
the held-out-jurisdiction test has only 88 anchors ($\pm$8.8\,pp
Hits@1 CI). The cross-encoder gain clears the CI band on all three
splits, but per-split absolute numbers should be read with those
sample sizes in mind. A secondary auto-classifier check
(Section~\ref{ssec:diagnostics}) bounds label noise at $<$2\% on the
confident-positive subset; expert multi-annotator review would give
a tighter bound.

\paragraph{The LLM baselines run on a sample, not the full test set.}
All three LLM rows in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} are evaluated on
the same stratified test-set sample (56 / 52 / 50 anchors for TIME /
EVENT / JURIS), with each anchor's full candidate pool scored.
Hits@1 95\% bootstrap CIs run $\pm$10--14\,pp across the LLM rows.
Sonnet 4.6 nearly closes the gap on TIME and JURIS (both within
sample noise) but trails by $+0.31$ on EVENT ($p = 0.004$). Running
the full benchmark ($\approx$\$200 closed-API) and adding
Opus / GPT-5 would tighten CIs and check whether the EVENT gap
holds at the frontier ceiling.

\paragraph{The model is specialised, and parent/subsidiary is a thin slice.} The cross-encoder underperforms zero-shot E5-small
on out-of-domain product matching (Walmart-Amazon, $-$5.9\,pp AUROC;
Section~\ref{ssec:diagnostics}); the fine-tuned representation is
specialized to lifecycle ER, not generic. The parent/subsidiary
negative class shows a regression (mean score 0.45 $\to$ 0.70 from
bi-encoder to v2 cross-encoder, worsening to 0.78 in v10), but with
only 221 negatives the effect-size estimate is not statistically
meaningful. Explicit multi-class modeling of related-but-distinct
entities is a v3 target.

\section{Conclusion}

\textsc{TemporalLink} is an open benchmark for lifecycle-aware
entity resolution on financial entities, built from public GLEIF,
SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata data and reproducible end-to-end on a
single MacBook in $\sim$2 hours. Hard negatives are mined to be
name-similar to each predecessor and true successors are by
construction name-different, so similarity-based baselines stay
at or below 0.55 AUROC on the time and event splits --- a property
of this benchmark's construction, not of name similarity in ER
generally. A 22M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder reaches 0.83--0.87
AUROC across all three splits (vs.\ $\leq$0.55 for every non-cross-encoder
method on time and event) and 56--75\% Hits@1, sitting 24--35\,pp
above the strongest bi-encoder, $p < 10^{-3}$ over Haiku and
gpt-oss-20b on every split; against Sonnet 4.6 the gap is decisive
only on EVENT ($p = 0.004$). Re-mining negatives shrinks the advantage 5--15\,pp but keeps
it $+13$ to $+38$\,pp above zero-shot; a fielded retrieve-then-rerank
simulation over the 3.3M-entity universe drops end-to-end Hits@1
to 15--30\% because bi-encoder Recall@20 caps at 33--50\%. The real
gains localise to M\&A, absorption, and breakup; pure renames are
already at 0.90 Hits@1 zero-shot.

weights, and the miner-robustness experiment at
\url{https://github.com/chang-07/er-research}; end-to-end repro
from raw GLEIF $\sim$2 hours on one MacBook.
