\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textsc{Sylvester}, a public benchmark for
\emph{lifecycle-aware} entity resolution on financial entities:
matching records across mergers, rebrands, spinoffs, and
jurisdiction migrations where the surface form has deliberately
changed. Built from public GLEIF Level-1/2, SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata,
it provides 125{,}519 labeled pairs over 3.3M entities across three
held-out evaluation regimes (time, event type, jurisdiction). Our
central finding: a 22M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder fine-tuned on
this data reaches 0.83--0.87 AUROC across all three held-out regimes
and 57--61\,\% Hits@1 on the time and event splits ($\pm$2.0--2.3\,pp
95\,\% CI), 27--35\,pp above the strongest bi-encoder and, under a
uniform single-pair prompting protocol, above all six zero-shot LLMs
tested (four organizations) on those splits.
The lift concentrates on M\&A, absorption, and breakup events, where
the post-event surface form retains little of the predecessor's name.

Two caveats bound this result. The benchmark deliberately mines
name-similar hard negatives against name-different positives; the
near-chance AUROC of most similarity baselines on the time and event
splits is a construction choice, not a claim about name matching in
ER generally. An \emph{inverted}-similarity baseline exploits it for
64--70\,\% Hits@1 on the benchmark's own candidate pools, yet
collapses on candidates from an unrelated miner and on candidates
retrieved from the full universe --- where, among the methods we
test, only the cross-encoder stays strong. In a fielded
retrieve-then-rerank simulation over the full 3.3M-entity universe,
end-to-end Hits@1 falls to 15--29\,\% because bi-encoder Recall@20
caps at 33--50\,\%; the bottleneck is the first stage, not the
reranker. Results reproduce end-to-end from code, released splits,
and weights on one MacBook in $\sim$2 hours.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Twitter Inc.\ became X Corp. Facebook became Meta. Sprint was absorbed into
T-Mobile. Every fintech engineer, quant researcher, and compliance
analyst has a story of a pipeline silently breaking on one of these
transitions. The records
look completely different on the surface --- different legal names, different
tickers, sometimes different jurisdictions and legal forms --- yet they
describe the same continuing economic entity.

Pair-matching ER --- including LLM-based methods that achieve 99\%
F1 on point-in-time records~\cite{opensanctions2026} --- treats each
record as a snapshot of identity. Temporal entity-linking benchmarks
such as TempEL~\cite{tempel2022} address mention-to-KB disambiguation
against an evolving knowledge base, a different task. Financial KG
construction~\cite{finreflectkg2025} works at a single
time slice. To the best of our knowledge no public benchmark targets
the specific question we care about: \emph{given two records observed
at different times, do they describe the same economic entity,
accounting for any lifecycle event that may have occurred between
them?}

A natural concern: the benchmark is engineered against name similarity
by construction --- negatives are mined name-similar while lifecycle
successors are, by definition, name-different. This is deliberate,
documented (\S\ref{ssec:splits}, \S\ref{ssec:anti}), and
operationalized: we report the inverted-similarity exploit as its own
baseline. The question the benchmark asks is the discriminative
sub-problem a two-stage pipeline faces after a name-aware retriever
narrows the field: \emph{given a name-similar candidate pool, what
architecture correctly identifies the true lifecycle successor?}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An open benchmark} (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}):
        125{,}519 labeled pairs over 3.3M GLEIF Level-1 entities,
        hard negatives in four classes backed by 474K GLEIF Level-2
        relationships, and three held-out regimes (time, event type,
        jurisdiction). Construction choices and their scope of
        generalizability are documented throughout
        (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}, \S\ref{sec:limitations}).

  \item \textbf{A diagnostic finding} (\S\ref{sec:experiments}):
        most similarity-based methods score near-chance AUROC on the time
        and event splits because negatives are mined to be name-similar
        while positives are name-different by construction---not a
        claim about ER generally. The construction cuts both ways:
        \emph{inverted} name similarity reaches 0.64--0.70 Hits@1 on
        those splits, yet collapses on the TF-IDF pool, the fielded
        pool, and renames (\S\ref{ssec:anti}). A 22M-parameter
        cross-encoder is the only method strong across all of these
        conditions (0.83--0.87 AUROC; 24--35\,pp Hits@1 over the best
        bi-encoder), with gains concentrated on M\&A and absorption
        events where the post-event name comes from the acquirer. The advantage survives
        a mechanically different miner ($-$2--13\,pp but $+$13--39\,pp
        above zero-shot on all cross-pool cells; \S\ref{ssec:robustness}).

  \item \textbf{The deployment bottleneck is retrieval, not reranking}
        (\S\ref{ssec:robustness}): in a fielded simulation over
        3.3M entities, end-to-end Hits@1 falls to 15--29\,\%
        because bi-encoder Recall@20 caps at 33--50\,\%. The
        cross-encoder reranks effectively (46--58\,\% conditional
        Hits@1 when given the right candidates); the system breaks at
        the first stage, not the second.
\end{enumerate}

An enriched-prompt L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble lifts Hits@1 a further
9--11\,pp on time/event (Table~\ref{tab:v7}); baseline comparisons
use the single fine-tuned L-6 cross-encoder.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Entity resolution and record linkage.} Classical
ER~\cite{fellegisunter1969} matches records via similarity over
structured fields. Modern PLM cross-encoders on serialized records
(GraLMatch~\cite{gralmatch2025}) are now standard, and the
OpenSanctions Pairs corpus~\cite{opensanctions2026} benchmarks LLM
matching at 755K pairs. All adopt a \emph{snapshot} framing, not
lifecycle-spanning matches.

\paragraph{Temporal entity linking.} TempEL~\cite{tempel2022}
time-stratifies entity linking against an evolving Wikipedia, but
the task is mention-to-KB disambiguation, not record-to-record
matching over legal entities with structured lifecycle events.

\paragraph{Financial entity knowledge graphs.} Recent work at the ACM
Conference on AI in Finance constructs financial-filing knowledge
graphs (FinReflectKG~\cite{finreflectkg2025}) at a single time
slice; the lifecycle-bridge question is orthogonal.

\section{The Sylvester Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Data sources}

We construct \textsc{Sylvester} entirely from open, persistently-licensed
sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-1 LEI-CDF concatenated file.} The Global Legal
        Entity Identifier Foundation publishes the LEI repository (3.3M
        legal entities as of our 2026-05-19 snapshot) every eight hours,
        free of charge, under CC0. Each \texttt{LEIRecord} includes the
        entity's legal name, jurisdiction, legal form, registered and
        headquarters addresses, registration metadata, and crucially the
        \texttt{LegalEntityEvents} container --- a structured log of
        completed and in-progress lifecycle events with effective and
        recorded dates --- and a \texttt{SuccessorEntity} field that
        explicitly links predecessor to successor at the LEI level.

  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-2 Relationship Records.} 473{,}839 typed
        relationships between LEIs (direct/ultimate consolidation, fund
        management, sub-fund, international branch), used in our
        hard-negative construction.

  \item \textbf{Wikidata.} High-profile M\&A via SPARQL on the P156
        (\emph{followed by}) property, restricted to businesses; a
        marquee supplement and cross-validation source.

  \item \textbf{SEC EDGAR.} The 10{,}354-record
        \texttt{company\_tickers.json} file gives a free name $\leftrightarrow$
        CIK crosswalk that maps to GLEIF for the U.S.\ public-company
        subset (3{,}334 confident name-match links).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positive labels}

Our gold-standard positives are GLEIF \texttt{SuccessorEntity} links: explicit
predecessor-LEI $\to$ successor-LEI pairs maintained by GLEIF's network of
Local Operating Units. The 2026-05-19 snapshot contains 50{,}202 records
with a non-null \texttt{SuccessorEntity} element; 33{,}645 of these have a
resolvable \texttt{SuccessorLEI} (the rest reference successors that
never registered an LEI and are excluded).

\paragraph{Label quality validation.} A five-class auto-classifier on
the successor pairs (using documented event types, normalized name
delta, jurisdiction, legal form) yields: \texttt{strong\_real}
(documented event + name change) 25{,}428 (75.6\%);
\texttt{likely\_real\_name\_changed} 3{,}330 (9.9\%);
\texttt{likely\_real\_event\_only} 1{,}704 (5.1\%); \texttt{ambiguous}
1{,}355 (4.0\%); \textbf{\texttt{likely\_administrative}} 1{,}828
(5.4\%, filtered out). We retain the first four classes (94.6\%),
yielding 31{,}817 plausibly-real predecessors (31{,}824 positive
pairs; some predecessors have multiple successors).

\subsection{Hard negatives}

For each positive predecessor we mine up to 80 hard negatives drawn from
four classes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (221 pairs) --- LEIs that share a
        Level-2 relationship-record edge (consolidation,
        fund-management, sub-fund) with the
        predecessor. These look related but are legally distinct.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} (10{,}208) --- distinct LEIs with
        identical normalized legal names in the same jurisdiction:
        coincidental name collision vs.\ true lifecycle linkage.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (10{,}703) --- distinct LEIs
        with identical normalized name in a different country.
  \item \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (232{,}601) --- fuzzy
        fallback: distinct LEIs in the same country sharing $\geq 2$
        content tokens after normalization, ranked by Jaccard. Covers the
        long tail when name-exact collisions are sparse.
\end{itemize}

After deduplication: 253{,}733 candidate (predecessor, hard-negative) pairs
covering 20{,}138 of 31{,}817 predecessors ($\sim$63\%). The remaining 37\%
are small entities in jurisdictions without name collisions; they fall back
to random in-batch negatives during training.

\subsection{Evaluation splits}
\label{ssec:splits}

For each positive we identify its \emph{anchor lifecycle event} --- the
highest-priority COMPLETED event on the predecessor's record by
type-priority then effective date. We then assign each positive (and its
associated negatives) to one of three split regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-out time:} anchor effective date $<$ 2025-01-01
        (train) vs.\ $\geq$ 2025-01-01 (test). Completed-event volume
        rises $\sim$5$\times$ from 2022, when GLEIF tightened event
        reporting (distribution figure in the released repo), so the
        effective window is 2022--2026. Test composition:
        3{,}448 positives, ${>}$2{,}000 anchors with at least one positive.
  \item \textbf{Held-out event type:} train on M\&A,
        rename, legal-form change; test on spinoff, demerger,
        breakup, absorption. The test set is heavily dominated by
        \emph{absorption} (2{,}609 of 2{,}760 anchors, 94.5\%);
        demerger (120), breakup (25), and spinoff (6) are present but
        statistically thin; per-event-type metrics within the split
        are reported in \S\ref{sec:experiments}.
  \item \textbf{Held-out jurisdiction:} train on US + GB + DE; test on
        SG + JP + BR. Test composition: 88 anchors, 161 positives ---
        the smallest split; its headline numbers carry
        $\sim$$\pm$8.5\,pp 95\% bootstrap CIs (vs.\
        $\sim$$\pm$2.0--2.3\,pp on time/event).
\end{itemize}

Each positive gets up to 5 hard negatives if its splits are all
\texttt{train}, or up to 10 if any split places it in \texttt{test}, yielding
125{,}519 final pair rows.


\paragraph{Wikidata marquee supplement.} Released alongside the
benchmark: 302 high-profile M\&A pairs from Wikidata P156
both-sides-resolved to GLEIF, 283 not captured by GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} (Sprint\,$\to$\,T-Mobile,
Wyeth\,$\to$\,Pfizer, Interbrew\,$\to$\,AB-InBev); not used in the
headline evaluation.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We present two methods at different cost-accuracy points: a bi-encoder
(\S\ref{ssec:bi}) that embeds each side independently and scores by
cosine --- cheap, but hemmed in by the benchmark's anti-correlation
property (name similarity anti-predicts the label here; \S\ref{ssec:anti}) ---
and a cross-encoder (\S\ref{ssec:ce}) that reads both records together,
4--10$\times$ more expensive per pair, producing the headline result.

\subsection{Bi-encoder}
\label{ssec:bi}

We fine-tune \texttt{intfloat/e5-small-v2} (33M params, 384-dim) via
supervised contrastive learning with state features folded into the
text prompt: \texttt{"query: } $n_\ell$ \texttt{| jurisdiction: }
$c_\ell$ \texttt{| form: } $f_\ell$\texttt{"}. Training uses the
26{,}297 positives in the strict no-leakage intersection of all
three split regimes (one hard neg per anchor), MultipleNegativesRanking
loss (InfoNCE; in-batch + explicit hard neg as negatives), 3 epochs,
batch 32, AdamW lr$=2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup, $\sim$6 min on
Apple-silicon (MPS).
Ablation variants in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}: \texttt{method\_no\_hard}
(in-batch negs only), \texttt{method\_v2\_full} (adds E5-cosine
hard negs + 1{,}372 transitive positives). Loss is contrastive; the
cross-encoder below uses BCE on the logit --- different objectives by
architectural convention, not strictly controlled.

\subsection{Cross-encoder}
\label{ssec:ce}

A bi-encoder must place each record at a fixed point in embedding
space, yet the event generating each positive deliberately changes
the record's surface form --- it is asked to keep cosine-close two
records that look very different on the page. A cross-encoder reads
both records jointly and can compare specific tokens between them.

We fine-tune a second-stage cross-encoder that ingests
$(\text{record}_A, \text{record}_B)$ as a single concatenated sequence
and emits a single relevance logit. The backbone is the
22M-parameter \texttt{ms-marco-\allowbreak MiniLM-L-6-v2} cross-encoder
from HuggingFace; training uses the same positive set as the
bi-encoder (including transitives) plus a 4:1 sample of E5-cosine hard
negatives per positive, with positives labeled $1.0$ and negatives
$0.0$.
Loss: standard binary cross-entropy on the logit. 2 epochs, batch 32, lr
$2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup steps. The extensions in
\S\ref{ssec:main} hold architecture/optimizer constant and modify only
the serialized text, the training positives, and the backbone.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} Scoring all 125{,}519 pairs in the benchmark
takes $\approx$80 seconds on MPS. In deployment the cross-encoder
would re-rank a retriever's top-$K$ (the standard two-stage IR
pattern); the benchmark's fixed 5--10-negative pools make direct
full scoring feasible, and Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reflects
that setting.

\paragraph{Hard-negative re-mining.}
\label{ssec:hard_negs}
The initial token-overlap fallback (92\% of negs) injected enough label
noise that explicit hard-negs slightly underperformed in-batch alone.
Replacing it with E5-cosine top-20 same-country candidates yields
652{,}764 total negs across 31{,}714 predecessors (vs.\ 253{,}733 before;
coverage $\sim$20K $\to$ 31{,}714) and erases the ``hard-negs hurt''
anomaly for the bi-encoder, while noticeably helping the cross-encoder.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four similarity baselines:
\texttt{exact\_name} (normalized legal-name equality),
\texttt{token\_set} (Jaccard over name tokens),
\texttt{tfidf\_char} (char-3gram TF-IDF cosine), and
\texttt{sentence\_encoder} (zero-shot E5-small-v2 cosine --- the
method's backbone without fine-tuning). We also report six zero-shot
LLM pairwise baselines under one protocol: one API call per pair,
byte-identical prompts, a 0--10 integer rubric,
\texttt{temperature=0} where exposed. Five API models spanning three
organizations run at \emph{full} test coverage (26{,}564 pairs
each): \textbf{Claude Haiku 4.5}, \textbf{Sonnet 4.6},
\textbf{Opus 4.7}, \textbf{Gemini 3.1 Pro}, \textbf{DeepSeek V4 Pro}
(thinking disabled); \textbf{gpt-oss-20b} (a fourth organization)
runs locally on a 56/52/50-anchor sample
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results} footnotes).

Paired permutation tests on per-anchor expected Hits@1 separate the
comparisons cleanly. The single L-6 cross-encoder beats all five API
models on TIME and EVENT at $p < 10^{-4}$ ($+15$ to $+37$\,pp). On
the 88-anchor JURIS split it leads Haiku, Gemini, and DeepSeek
($p \leq 0.018$), is within noise of Sonnet ($p = 0.14$), and is
statistically indistinguishable from Opus 4.7, which holds the
higher point estimate (0.767 vs.\ 0.750, $p = 0.74$). The
L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble beats Opus on TIME/EVENT ($p < 10^{-4}$);
JURIS is a coin flip (0.784 vs.\ 0.767). Figure~\ref{fig:llm_panel}
shows the panel: zero-shot quality is strikingly
vendor-heterogeneous --- every Claude model outscores both other
vendors' frontier models on every split, including Haiku, the
cheapest model tested; \S\ref{sec:limitations} examines the
elicitation-format confound behind part of this spread.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report per-anchor retrieval metrics (Hits@1, MRR) computed by
ranking each anchor's candidates by score, and AUROC for the pair-wise
classification framing; Hits@3, AUPRC, and threshold-tuned F1 ship with
the released per-run metrics. Discrete scorers tie frequently within
a pool, so Hits@k/MRR are exact \emph{expectations over uniformly
random tie-breaking} (a fixed tie order is biased). Per-anchor
metrics cover \emph{eligible} anchors --- pool size $\geq 2$ with
the gold present. Because 37\% of predecessors have no mined hard
negative (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}), their singleton pools are
excluded: e.g., 1{,}612 of 2{,}760 EVENT-test anchors are eligible.

\paragraph{Extensions: enriched prompt + L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble.}
Stacking four modifications on the headline cross-encoder ---
prompt enrichment (GLEIF Level-2 parent/child names, founding year,
ISO country expansion, category), Wikidata-LEI mining (259 GLEIF
positives recovered, 49 cross-jurisdiction), multi-seed averaging,
and ensembling with an L-12-backbone variant --- lifts Hits@1 a
further $+9$ to $+11$\,pp on time/event ($+3$ on jurisdiction,
inside its CI band; Table~\ref{tab:v7}).
The ensemble reaches 0.700 / 0.677 / 0.784 Hits@1; the headline
result reported against baselines remains the single L-6
cross-encoder, so that comparison stays apples-to-apples. A negative
result is worth recording: symmetric scoring (averaging $s(a,b)$ and
$s(b,a)$) hurts $-0.9 / -1.9 / -9.1$\,pp, so the cross-encoder really
does learn a directional predecessor$\to$successor representation.

\paragraph{Backbone size does not break the bi-encoder ceiling.}
Scaling E5-small (33M) to E5-base (110M) leaves TIME/EVENT Hits@1
unchanged ($-0.2 / -1.2$\,pp) and lifts JURIS $+2.3$\,pp: the

embeddings for lexically-different pairs are not a problem more
parameters fix.


\subsection{The construction cuts both ways: an anti-similarity baseline}
\label{ssec:anti}

\texttt{token\_set}, \texttt{tfidf\_char}, and
\texttt{sentence\_encoder} all land below 0.5 AUROC on the
held-out-time and held-out-event-type splits
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The cause is mechanical: hard
negatives are deliberately name-similar to the predecessor while
positives are by construction name-different, so on this candidate
distribution name similarity is \emph{evidence against} a true
successor link. So we report the mirror image as its own baseline:
\texttt{anti-token\_set} ranks candidates by \emph{negated} Jaccard,
and on the curated pool it is strong --- 0.700 / 0.638 / 0.516
Hits@1, \emph{above} the single L-6 cross-encoder on time and event
--- because picking the least name-similar candidate exploits the
construction.

The exploit does not survive any change of conditions. On
\textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF} it collapses to chance (0.25 / 0.25 /
0.21 Hits@1, AUROC $\approx$ 0.5) while the cross-encoders hold
0.53--0.66 (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}); on the fielded pool it
drops to 9--18\,\% conditional Hits@1 vs.\ 46--58\,\% for the
cross-encoder; per event it is a one-way bet on acquirer-renaming
--- 0.72 on M\&A but 0.10 on pure renames and 0.35 on legal-form
changes, where the cross-encoder scores 0.90 and 0.85. The
cross-encoder also wins pairwise AUROC on every split (0.83--0.87
vs.\ 0.68--0.81) and Hits@1 on JURIS ($+$23\,pp), and the
L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble matches the exploit on TIME (0.700) and
beats it on EVENT and JURIS.
Curated-pool Hits@1 alone can therefore be gamed; the
cross-encoder's claim is being strong \emph{simultaneously} on the
curated pool, the alternative-miner pool, the fielded pool, and all
event types. The mechanism is joint attention: asking whether the
\emph{differences} between two records look like a lifecycle-event
pattern, not how similar two strings are.

\subsection{Where the lift is real: per-event-type breakdown}
\label{ssec:per_event}

Aggregate Hits@1 hides large per-event-type variation. The
cross-encoder does real work on M\&A, absorption, and breakup
events, where the post-event name comes from the acquirer rather
than the target and string-similarity cannot bridge the gap. On
pure renames it does essentially nothing: those are already at
0.90 Hits@1 zero-shot.

\paragraph{On the held-out-time split (Figure~\ref{fig:per_event}),
the cross-encoder's gain is concentrated in the operationally hard
events.} Pure renames (\texttt{CHANGE\_LEGAL\_NAME}, $n=10$) are
essentially solved by every method including zero-shot E5-small
(0.90 Hits@1, no lift). The headline gains come from
\texttt{MERGERS\_AND\_ACQUISITIONS} ($n=1{,}506$: 0.18 zero-shot
$\to$ 0.62 L-6 $\to$ 0.71 ensemble) and \texttt{ABSORPTION} ($n=461$:
0.23 $\to$ 0.53 $\to$ 0.65), where the post-event name comes from
the acquirer. The ensemble also more than doubles zero-shot on the
small but operationally critical \texttt{BREAKUP} subset ($n=7$:
0.14 $\to$ 0.71).

\paragraph{On the held-out-event split, ``held-out event type'' really
means ``held-out absorption.''} The held-out-event test set is 94.5\%
\texttt{ABSORPTION} anchors (\S\ref{ssec:splits}); within the
Hits@1-eligible subset, \texttt{DEMERGER}, \texttt{BREAKUP}, and
\texttt{SPINOFF} contribute $n=42$, $n=20$, and $n=4$ respectively. We report
Table~\ref{tab:event_split_breakdown} so that the contribution of each
held-out type is visible separately. The cross-encoder lift is
consistent across the absorption-like types (\texttt{ABSORPTION}: 0.22
$\to$ 0.57, \texttt{DEMERGER}: 0.29 $\to$ 0.55, \texttt{BREAKUP}: 0.15
$\to$ 0.40); the \texttt{SPINOFF} cell is statistically meaningless at
$n=4$. The aggregate ``+34 pp Hits@1 over zero-shot E5 on the event split'' number is
therefore primarily a transfer claim about absorption, not a strong
claim about spinoffs.

\subsection{Robustness to candidate-pool shift}
\label{ssec:robustness}

The headline cross-encoder result is trained and (mostly) evaluated against
candidates from the same family of hard-negative miners --- v1
token-overlap and v2 E5-cosine both rank candidates by name-related signals,
and the cross-encoder trains on E5-cosine negs. The obvious question: does the gap
survive an evaluation pool built with a mechanically different miner?  If
yes, the cross-encoder advantage is a property of the method; if no, the
headline number is partly an artifact of how the negatives were chosen.

\paragraph{Setup.} We re-mine hard negatives with sparse char-3gram
TF-IDF cosine (same signal as the \texttt{tfidf\_char} baseline) ---
chosen because it has no learned representation and shares no
parameters with E5. The other three classes (parent/subsidiary,
same-name same/diff-country) are held constant. We call the resulting pair set \textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF}; splits
and positives are unchanged.
We train two cross-encoders: one on the E5-cosine hard negatives
(trn-E5), one on the TF-IDF negatives (trn-TF-IDF), and score each
on both pair pools. Note the released pool's negatives are
token-overlap-mined, so the headline configuration (E5-trained,
benchmark-pool evaluation) is itself already cross-miner; the only
miner-matched cell in the matrix is trn-TF-IDF on the TF-IDF pool.

\paragraph{What the 2$\times$2 tells us.} First, the cross-encoder \emph{architecture}
generalizes well across miners: the TF-IDF-trained cross-encoder evaluated on
the benchmark pool reaches 0.588 / 0.533 / 0.750 Hits@1 --- within 1--2\,pp of
the E5-trained model on TIME and EVENT (surpassing it by 4.5\,pp on the
thin JURIS split, consistent with its wide CI), despite never having
seen E5-mined candidates during training. The result is symmetric: the E5-trained model
on the TF-IDF pool likewise stays well above any non-cross-encoder method.

Second, there is a real but bounded in-distribution effect. The E5-trained
cross-encoder loses 2--13\,pp Hits@1 when moved to the TF-IDF evaluation pool
(0.600 $\to$ 0.545 on time; 0.705 $\to$ 0.571 on jurisdiction). Conversely,
the TF-IDF-trained model gains $+7$--$11$\,pp on the TF-IDF pool over the E5-trained one.
So aligning the training miner with the evaluation pool --- testable
only in the TF-IDF column --- is worth $+7$--$11$\,pp, and pool shift
costs the E5-trained model 2--13\,pp: non-trivial, but not the
headline gap.

Third, the cross-encoder gain over zero-shot E5 \emph{is robust} to
the miner: headline pairings show $+20$ to $+40$\,pp across all six
columns and swapped pairings still $+13$ to $+39$\,pp --- smaller,
but well outside any CI band. Joint encoding does real work on lifecycle ER --- but the absolute
size of the gain depends on how the candidate pool was mined, so the
``$+27$--$35$\,pp'' figure in the abstract should be read in that light.

\paragraph{Fielded retrieve-then-rerank.} The miner-robustness
experiment above shifts the mining signal but still draws candidates
from a curated negative pool. A real deployment pipeline does
something different: a first-stage retriever pulls the top-K
candidates from the full entity universe, then a second-stage model
reranks. We simulate that here. The trained
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} bi-encoder retrieves top-20 candidates per
test anchor from the full 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe, restricted to
the anchor's jurisdiction (the lightest filter a production system
would apply); the cross-encoder reranks. The filter itself is
nearly free: only 1.8--4.1\,\% of test positives have a successor
in a different country, so it caps recall at $\geq$96\,\% --- far
above the observed 33--50\,\%; the bottleneck is the embedding,
not the filter.

Bi-encoder Recall@20 is 33.2 / 39.6 / 49.7\% on TIME / EVENT /
JURIS. That is the hard ceiling for any reranker. When the bi-encoder
does retrieve the true successor, the L-6 cross-encoder picks it
\#1 with 46--58\% Hits@1 and the L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble reaches
53--60\% --- the ensemble's edge over the L-6 is modest once
distractors look genuinely lifecycle-plausible.
End-to-end deployment Hits@1 (recall $\times$ conditional,
un-retrieved truths counted as misses) is 15--29\% for the L-6
cross-encoder and 17--30\% for the ensemble. The drop from
57--61\% in-distribution on time/event (75\% on JURIS, $n=88$)
to 15--30\% deployment confirms rather than contradicts
\S\ref{ssec:bi}: the bi-encoder's fixed-embedding limit, which
fails to retrieve lifecycle-spanning successors by cosine
similarity, is exactly the bottleneck the anti-correlation
analysis predicts.

\subsection{Ablations}

\textbf{State features help modestly.} Ablating jurisdiction and
legal form from the prompt costs 1.5--2\,pp Hits@1 on every split;
the country token contributes most on held-out-jurisdiction, least
on held-out-event-type.

\textbf{Explicit hard negatives slightly \emph{hurt} in v1}
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). \texttt{method\_no\_hard} edges out
the v1 full model by 0.4--1.1\,pp Hits@1 on every split. The culprit
is the \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} class, 92\% of all
mined v1 hard negatives and the lowest-precision class --- the fuzzy
fallback injects label noise, and in-batch negatives from
MultipleNegativesRanking are cleaner on their own. This motivates
the v2 switch from token-overlap to E5-cosine mining, and pushes
back on the default assumption that more hard negatives is always
better.

\subsection{Additional diagnostics}
\label{ssec:diagnostics}

Four diagnostics; full tables ship with the released code.
\textbf{B-cubed cluster F1}~\cite{binette2024} on the test
partition is 0.92 / 0.90 / 0.91 for the cross-encoder vs.\
$\leq$0.47 for any bi-encoder (each of which collapses to one
giant cluster at the tuned threshold). Restricting to records in
true clusters of size $\geq 2$ --- 64\% of test records are
singletons --- gives a more honest 0.85 / 0.84 / 0.88.
\textbf{Calibration and selective prediction.} ECE (10-bin) is
0.20, Brier 0.18: the model is over-confident in its high-score
bin. A train-fitted temperature ($T\!\approx\!1.5$) trims Brier
only marginally and does not reduce ECE, so we report
uncalibrated scores. A selective-prediction~\cite{geifman2017} sweep on the ensemble hits
Hits@1 $\geq 0.94$ at 20\% coverage and 0.87--0.97 at 50\% across
the three splits; users who need high precision can just threshold
on the score (curve in repo).
\textbf{Cross-domain transfer.} Zero-shot Magellan Walmart-Amazon:
the cross-encoder \emph{loses} to its own zero-shot base encoder
by 5.9\,pp AUROC. The fine-tuned representation is specialized to
lifecycle-spanning legal-entity matching, not generic ER.
\textbf{Label quality, second opinion.} A rule-based re-classifier
--- orthogonal to the primary's documented-event-type evidence,
though still name-informed (name-stem similarity, cross-script
checks, legal-form continuity) --- agrees with the primary
auto-classifier on 98.7\% [95\% CI 97.0--100\%] of the 231/277
stratified-sampled positives it judges confidently; the remaining
46 (16.6\%) are marked uncertain. This bounds heuristically
detectable label noise below 2\% there; human expert review is
future work.

\subsection{Where each method is fooled}

Table~\ref{tab:neg_class} reports per-negative-class mean score
(higher = more often fooled). The fine-tuned bi-encoders roughly halve the
zero-shot E5 baseline's score on \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} and
\texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} negatives, but
\texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} remains a wall at $\approx$0.90 mean score: the bi-encoder
cannot reliably tell a real successor link from two distinct entities
sharing an exact normalized name in the same country.

The cross-encoder breaks that wall: \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country}
mean score drops from 0.90 (best bi-encoder) to 0.45, and the
L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble drops it further to 0.34. The ensemble also
nearly solves \texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (0.92 zero-shot
$\to$ 0.16) and \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (0.91 $\to$ 0.25). The one
regression is \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (0.45 bi-encoder $\to$ 0.70
L-6 cross-encoder $\to$ 0.78 ensemble): the joint encoder occasionally
treats parent--subsidiary edges as positive lifecycle bridges, and the
ensemble's prompt enrichment that injects GLEIF L2 parent/child names makes
the regression \emph{worse}, not better. With only 221 examples in
this class the headline impact is small, but the trend points at a
clear direction: explicit multi-class modeling of corporate-hierarchy
relationships.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Error analysis}

We sampled 100 \texttt{method\_full} failures on the held-out-time
test split (positives not ranked first) and categorized them via
simple text features: 66 mixed/other, 19 where anchor and true
successor share \emph{no} tokens, 8 with substring overlap with a
wrong candidate, and 7 exact normalized-name collisions with a
wrong candidate.

The 19 ``no shared tokens'' cases are the hardest: the true successor
of a name like \texttt{Nima N.V.} (NL) --- here
\texttt{Beheermaatschappij Arros B.V.} --- shares zero meaningful tokens with
either the predecessor or the wrong top-1 candidate \texttt{NIMA B.V.}. No
similarity-based representation can solve these; they require world
knowledge or explicit graph context (board overlap, regulatory filings,
press releases). One representative failure: anchor
\texttt{DB HR Solutions GmbH} (DE), true successor
\texttt{DEUTSCHE BANK AKTIENGESELLSCHAFT}, top-1 wrong
\texttt{HR Solutions GmbH} --- resolving ``DB'' to Deutsche Bank is
external knowledge a 33M-parameter encoder is unlikely to memorize
from name strings alone.

\paragraph{Residual JURIS failures cluster on sibling consolidation.} Of the 19 / 88 anchors the ensemble mis-ranks on JURIS,
$\geq$11 are sibling consolidations (e.g., 9 ``\texttt{EPIC <port>
PTE.\ LTD.}'' Singapore ships into \texttt{BW EPIC KOSAN VESSELS
ONE}; the model returns sibling \texttt{EPIC} vessels); a second
cluster is Japanese-script name changes the ASCII tokenizer cannot
bridge (Daiwa-Sumigin $\to$ Mitsui-Sumitomo DS). Multilingual
pretraining plus ownership-graph context would address both ---
though \S\ref{sec:experiments} shows zero-shot prompting alone
is not the answer.

\subsection{Why this matters in practice}

The benchmark targets a failure mode common across financial
infrastructure. Stripe trains transaction and merchant embeddings at
scale~\cite{stripe_ai}; Ramp's accounting agents flag duplicate
vendors and invoices~\cite{ramp_agents2025}; Bloomberg's entity
backbone underpins its NLP and financial modeling
work~\cite{bloomberg_delphyne2025}. Each
of these pipelines faces the lifecycle-ER gap: entity records that
look completely different after a merger, rebrand, or absorption.
\textsc{Sylvester}
gives practitioners a public benchmark to evaluate whether their
system handles it.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{The ``anti-correlation'' finding is a construction artifact.}
Hard negatives in \textsc{Sylvester} are mined to be
name-similar to the predecessor; positives are by definition
name-different. The near-chance AUROC of most similarity baselines
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}) follows directly from that mining
design and does not generalize to ER outside this benchmark. The
finding motivates joint encoding here; it is not a universal claim
about name similarity in ER.

\paragraph{In-distribution headline overstates deployment.}
The headline cross-encoder Hits@1 (57--61\,\% on time/event;
75\,\% on JURIS, $n=88$, directional) is on the released
token-overlap-mined candidate pool; on a fielded retrieve-then-rerank pipeline that
draws candidates from the full 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}), end-to-end Hits@1 falls to 15--29\,\%
(L-6 cross-encoder) / 17--30\,\% (ensemble). The cause is upstream:
bi-encoder Recall@20 is 33--50\,\%, so the reranker often never sees
the true successor. The miner-robustness experiment shows the
cross-encoder gain itself is partly in-distribution but not purely a
within-miner artifact ($+13$ to $+39$\,pp cross-pool). A stronger first-stage
retriever --- a learned hybrid sparse-dense index, or a coarsely
pre-filtered cross-encoder shortlist --- is the highest-leverage
target for future work.

\paragraph{Seed variance and a sliver of mislabeled hard negatives.}
Bi-encoder Hits@1 over seeds 42/7/1337 was $0.251 \pm 0.005$
(TIME), $0.291 \pm 0.004$ (EVENT), $0.523 \pm 0.016$ (JURIS);
TIME/EVENT are stable, JURIS varies $\pm$1.6\,pp consistent with
its small test sample ($n = 88$); cross-encoder multi-seed
retraining behaves similarly. Separately, $205/93{,}695 = 0.22\%$ of hard negatives
appear in GLEIF's \texttt{SuccessorEntity}; the cleaned variant
\texttt{pairs\_clean.parquet} drops them with $\leq$0.2\,pp impact.

\paragraph{Reporting / survivorship bias + thin slices.} GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} is voluntarily populated, biasing positives
toward well-counseled regulated entities. The held-out-event test set
is 94.5\% absorption (the label oversells event-type generality), and
the held-out-jurisdiction test has only 88 anchors ($\pm$8.5\,pp
Hits@1 CI). The cross-encoder gain clears the CI band on all three
splits, but per-split absolute numbers should be read with those
sample sizes in mind. A 277-pair second-classifier check
(\S\ref{ssec:diagnostics}) bounds detectable label noise at $<$2\% on the
confident-positive subset; expert human review would give
a stronger bound. Splits are pair-level, not entity-disjoint
(32\,\%\,/\,21\,\% of time-split test candidates\,/\,gold
successors appear in training pairs; JURIS $<$1\,\%), though L-6
Hits@1 shows no seen-entity advantage (unseen 0.62 vs.\ seen 0.57
TIME; 0.56 vs.\ 0.58 EVENT, both n.s.); entity-disjoint splits are
a stricter future design.

\paragraph{LLM baselines: what the discrete scale hides.} Discrete
elicitation caps what ranking can express: models forfeit
13--48\,pp Hits@1 to within-pool score ties. Gemini 3.1 Pro scores
every candidate in a pool \emph{identically} on 55\% of TIME anchors
--- oracle tie-breaking would give it 0.72 vs.\ 0.23 measured ---
while Sonnet/Opus collapse only $\sim$5\% of pools; the
cross-encoder's continuous scores pay no tie tax. Finer elicitation
(wider scales, log-probs, comparative tie-breaks) may recover part
of this. Prompt format also moves results materially (batched
multi-candidate scoring: Sonnet 0.43 TIME Hits@1 vs.\ 0.30
single-pair), and cross-vendor gaps may partly reflect differential
training exposure to GLEIF-derived text. The cost gap stands:
0.64\,ms/pair after one-time training vs.\ $\sim$1\,s/pair metered.

\paragraph{The model is specialized, and parent/subsidiary is a thin slice.} The cross-encoder underperforms zero-shot E5-small
on out-of-domain product matching (Walmart-Amazon, $-$5.9\,pp AUROC;
\S\ref{ssec:diagnostics}); the fine-tuned representation is
specialized to lifecycle ER, not generic. The parent/subsidiary
negative class shows a regression (mean score 0.45 $\to$ 0.70 from
bi-encoder to L-6 cross-encoder, worsening to 0.78 in the ensemble), but with
only 221 negatives the effect-size estimate is not statistically
meaningful. Explicit multi-class modeling of related-but-distinct
entities is a clear direction for future work.

\section{Conclusion}

\textsc{Sylvester} is an open benchmark for lifecycle-aware
entity resolution on financial entities, built from public GLEIF,
SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata data. Hard negatives are mined to be
name-similar to each predecessor and true successors are by
construction name-different, so similarity-based baselines stay
at or below 0.55 AUROC on the time and event splits --- a property
of this benchmark's construction, not of name similarity in ER
generally (inverted similarity scores 0.64--0.70 Hits@1 on-pool,
collapsing off-pool; \S\ref{ssec:anti}).
A 22M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder reaches 0.83--0.87
AUROC across all three splits and 57--61\,\% Hits@1 on the time and event
splits (75\,\% on held-out jurisdiction, $n=88$, directional),
24--35\,pp above the strongest bi-encoder and above every zero-shot
LLM tested on time/event ($p < 10^{-4}$ vs.\ each API model); on
jurisdiction only Opus 4.7 outpoints it (0.767 vs.\ 0.750, n.s.).
Re-mining negatives shrinks the advantage 2--13\,pp but keeps
it $+13$ to $+39$\,pp above zero-shot; a fielded retrieve-then-rerank
simulation over 3.3M entities drops end-to-end Hits@1 to 15--30\%
because bi-encoder Recall@20 caps at 33--50\%. The real
gains concentrate in M\&A, absorption, and breakup; pure renames
($n{=}10$) are already at 0.90 Hits@1 zero-shot.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code, parsers, predictions, and trained
weights: \url{https://github.com/chang-07/er-research}; end-to-end repro
from raw GLEIF $\sim$2 hours on one MacBook.
