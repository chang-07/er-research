\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textsc{Sylvester}, a public benchmark for
\emph{lifecycle-aware} entity resolution on financial entities:
matching records across mergers, rebrands, spinoffs, and
jurisdiction migrations where the surface form has deliberately
changed. Built from public GLEIF Level-1/2, SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata,
it provides 125{,}519 labeled pairs over 3.3M entities across three
held-out evaluation regimes (time, event type, jurisdiction), with a
three-pool evaluation protocol whose job is to separate methods that
learn the pool from methods that learn the task: the released pool,
a pool from an unrelated miner, and candidates retrieved from the
full universe. The protocol has teeth: the benchmark deliberately mines
name-similar negatives against name-different positives, and the
methods that exploit this --- inverted name similarity (64--70\%
Hits@1 on time/event) and a name-feature GBM (0.73--0.84, the best
name-input scores we observe) --- collapse on the other two
pools.
Among methods restricted to name and registration-state inputs, a
22M-parameter MiniLM cross-encoder is the only one that transfers:
0.82--0.87 AUROC across the three regimes, 57--61\% Hits@1 on
time/event ($\pm$2.0--2.3\,pp 95\% CI), 24--35\,pp above the
strongest bi-encoder, and, under a uniform single-pair protocol,
above all six zero-shot LLMs tested (four organizations) on the
two large splits.
But the ceiling is not architectural: a gradient-boosted matcher
given the \emph{full} structured registry record (liveness,
addresses, previous names, dates --- fields no name-only method
sees) transfers across every pool as well, beating the
cross-encoder on-pool by 18\,pp ($p<10^{-4}$), within noise of
the best neural system on retrieved candidates, at negligible
inference cost.
The lifecycle signal lives in the record, not the name.
End-to-end, every reranker is capped at 15--33\% Hits@1 by
33--50\% retrieval recall: the bottleneck is retrieval.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Twitter Inc.\ became X Corp. Facebook became Meta. Sprint was absorbed into
T-Mobile. Pipelines silently breaking on such transitions is a
familiar failure mode across fintech engineering, quant research,
and compliance. The records
look completely different on the surface --- different legal names, different
tickers, sometimes different jurisdictions and legal forms --- yet they
describe the same continuing economic entity.

Pair-matching ER --- including LLM-based methods that achieve 99\%
F1 on point-in-time records~\cite{opensanctions2026} --- treats each
record as a snapshot of identity. Temporal entity-linking benchmarks
such as TempEL~\cite{tempel2022} address mention-to-KB disambiguation
against an evolving knowledge base, a different task. Financial KG
construction~\cite{finreflectkg2025} works at a single
time slice. To the best of our knowledge no public benchmark targets
the specific question we care about: \emph{given two records observed
at different times, do they describe the same economic entity,
accounting for any lifecycle event that may have occurred between
them?}

A natural concern: the benchmark is engineered against name similarity
by construction --- negatives are mined name-similar while lifecycle
successors are, by definition, name-different. This is deliberate,
documented (\S\ref{ssec:splits}, \S\ref{ssec:anti}), and measured:
we report the inverted-similarity exploit as its own baseline. The question the benchmark asks is the discriminative
sub-problem a two-stage pipeline faces after a name-aware retriever
narrows the field: \emph{given a name-similar candidate pool,
what identifies the true lifecycle successor?}
(Figure~\ref{fig:task}.)

\needspace{3\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An open benchmark} (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}):
        125{,}519 labeled pairs over 3.3M GLEIF Level-1 entities,
        hard negatives in four classes (the parent/subsidiary class
        drawn from GLEIF Level-2 relationship records), and three
        held-out regimes (time, event type, jurisdiction), with
        construction choices and their scope documented throughout.

  \item \textbf{A three-pool diagnostic, and what it separates}
        (\S\ref{sec:experiments}): negatives are mined name-similar
        while positives are name-different by construction --- not a
        claim about ER generally --- so \emph{inverted} name
        similarity and a name-feature GBM post the best
        name-input on-pool scores we observe, then collapse on the
        two other pools
        (\S\ref{ssec:anti}). Among name+state-input methods a
        22M-parameter cross-encoder is the only one that transfers
        (24--35\,pp Hits@1 over the best bi-encoder, above all six
        zero-shot LLMs on TIME/EVENT; gains concentrated on M\&A
        and absorption). But given the \emph{full} structured
        record, a gradient-boosted matcher transfers everywhere too
        and beats the cross-encoder on-pool by 18\,pp
        ($p<10^{-4}$; \S\ref{ssec:anti}): the transferable signal is in the
        record's structured fields, not the name --- and no name-only
        method, neural or not, can recover it.

  \item \textbf{The deployment bottleneck is retrieval, not
        reranking} (\S\ref{ssec:robustness}): fielded over 3.3M
        entities, end-to-end Hits@1 falls to 15--33\% because
        Recall@20 caps at 33--50\%, consistent with the blocking
        literature's focus on candidate
        generation~\cite{uniblocker2024}. The rerankers themselves
        are effective (46--66\% conditional Hits@1); the system
        breaks at the first stage.
\end{enumerate}

An enriched-prompt L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble lifts Hits@1 a further
9--11\,pp on time/event (Table~\ref{tab:v7}); baseline comparisons
use the single fine-tuned L-6 cross-encoder.

\section{Related Work}

Classical ER~\cite{fellegisunter1969} matches records via
similarity over structured fields; modern PLM cross-encoders on
serialized records (Ditto~\cite{ditto2021},
GraLMatch~\cite{gralmatch2025}) are now standard, and the
OpenSanctions Pairs corpus~\cite{opensanctions2026} benchmarks LLM
matching at 755K pairs --- all under a \emph{snapshot} framing,
not lifecycle-spanning matches. Temporal record
linkage~\cite{temporalrl2011} pioneered matching records of
entities whose attributes drift over time, but predates both modern
encoders and open registry infrastructure.
AnyMatch~\cite{anymatch2024} shows small fine-tuned models rival
LLM matchers at a fraction of the cost, and WDC
Products~\cite{wdcproducts2024} studies how hard-negative
construction shapes benchmark difficulty --- the design axis
\S\ref{ssec:anti} interrogates here. TempEL~\cite{tempel2022}
time-stratifies entity linking against an evolving Wikipedia, but
that task is mention-to-KB disambiguation, not record-to-record
matching over legal entities. Financial-filing knowledge graphs
(FinReflectKG~\cite{finreflectkg2025}, from this conference) are
constructed at a single time slice; the lifecycle-bridge question
is orthogonal.

\section{The Sylvester Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Data sources}

We construct \textsc{Sylvester} entirely from open, persistently-licensed
sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-1 LEI-CDF.} The LEI repository (3.3M
        entities, 2026-05-19 snapshot), CC0, refreshed every eight
        hours. Each \texttt{LEIRecord} carries legal name,
        jurisdiction, legal form, addresses, registration metadata, a
        dated \texttt{LegalEntityEvents} log, and a
        \texttt{SuccessorEntity} field linking predecessor to
        successor by LEI.

  \item \textbf{GLEIF Level-2 Relationship Records.} 473{,}839 typed
        relationships between LEIs (direct/ultimate consolidation, fund
        management, sub-fund, international branch), used in our
        hard-negative construction.

  \item \textbf{Wikidata.} High-profile M\&A via SPARQL on the P156
        (\emph{followed by}) property, restricted to businesses; a
        marquee supplement and cross-validation source.

  \item \textbf{SEC EDGAR.} \texttt{company\_tickers.json} (10{,}354
        records) gives a name$\leftrightarrow$CIK crosswalk to GLEIF
        for the U.S.\ public subset (3{,}334 confident links);
        released with the benchmark, not used in the evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positive labels}

Our gold-standard positives are GLEIF \texttt{SuccessorEntity} links: explicit
predecessor-LEI $\to$ successor-LEI pairs maintained by GLEIF's network of
Local Operating Units. The 2026-05-19 snapshot contains 50{,}202 records
with a non-null \texttt{SuccessorEntity} element; 33{,}645 of these have a
resolvable \texttt{SuccessorLEI} (the rest reference successors that
never registered an LEI and are excluded).

\paragraph{Label quality validation.} A five-class auto-classifier on
the successor pairs (using documented event types, normalized name
delta, jurisdiction, legal form) yields: \texttt{strong\_real}
(documented event + name change) 25{,}428;
\texttt{likely\_real\_name\_changed} 3{,}330;
\texttt{likely\_real\_event\_only} 1{,}704; \texttt{ambiguous}
1{,}355; \textbf{\texttt{likely\_administrative}} 1{,}828 (5.4\%,
filtered out). We retain the first four classes (94.6\%;
\texttt{ambiguous} stays because every link is still LOU-curated),
yielding 31{,}817 plausibly-real predecessors (31{,}824 positive
pairs; some predecessors have multiple successors).

\subsection{Hard negatives}

For each positive predecessor we mine up to 80 hard negatives drawn from
four classes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{parent\_subsidiary} (221 pairs) --- LEIs that share a
        Level-2 relationship-record edge (consolidation,
        fund-management, sub-fund) with the
        predecessor. These look related but are legally distinct.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} (10{,}208) --- distinct LEIs with
        identical normalized legal names in the same jurisdiction:
        coincidental name collision vs.\ true lifecycle linkage.
  \item \texttt{same\_name\_diff\_country} (10{,}703) --- distinct LEIs
        with identical normalized name in a different country.
  \item \texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} (232{,}601) --- fuzzy
        fallback: distinct LEIs in the same country sharing $\geq 2$
        content tokens after normalization, ranked by Jaccard. Covers the
        long tail when name-exact collisions are sparse.
\end{itemize}

After deduplication: 253{,}733 candidate (predecessor, hard-negative) pairs
covering 20{,}192 of 31{,}817 predecessors ($\sim$63\%). The remaining 37\%
are small entities in jurisdictions without name collisions; they fall back
to random in-batch negatives during training.

\subsection{Evaluation splits}
\label{ssec:splits}

For each positive we identify its \emph{anchor lifecycle event} --- the
highest-priority COMPLETED event on the predecessor's record by
type-priority then effective date. We then assign each positive (and its
associated negatives) to one of three split regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-out time:} anchor effective date $<$ 2025-01-01
        (train) vs.\ $\geq$ 2025-01-01 (test); completed-event
        volume rises $\sim$4$\times$ in 2022 when GLEIF tightened
        reporting, so the effective window is 2022--2026. Test:
        3{,}448 positives, 2{,}007 Hits@1-eligible anchors
        (\S\ref{ssec:metrics}).
  \item \textbf{Held-out event type:} train on M\&A,
        rename, legal-form change; test on spinoff, demerger,
        breakup, absorption --- dominated by \emph{absorption}
        (2{,}609 of 2{,}760 anchors, 94.5\%), the rest
        statistically thin (\S\ref{ssec:per_event}).
  \item \textbf{Held-out jurisdiction:} train on US + GB + DE; test on
        SG + JP + BR. Test composition: 161 anchors, 161 positives, of which
        88 are Hits@1-eligible --- the smallest split; its headline numbers carry
        $\sim$$\pm$8.5\,pp 95\% bootstrap CIs (vs.\
        $\sim$$\pm$2.0--2.3\,pp on time/event).
\end{itemize}

We refer to these regimes as TIME, EVENT, and JURIS.
Each positive gets up to 5 hard negatives if its splits are all
\texttt{train}, or up to 10 if any split places it in \texttt{test}, yielding
125{,}519 final pair rows.


\paragraph{Wikidata marquee supplement.} Released alongside the
benchmark: 302 high-profile M\&A pairs from Wikidata P156
both-sides-resolved to GLEIF, 283 not captured by GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} (Sprint\,$\to$\,T-Mobile,
Wyeth\,$\to$\,Pfizer, Interbrew\,$\to$\,AB-InBev); not used in the
headline evaluation.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We present two methods at different cost-accuracy points: a bi-encoder
(\S\ref{ssec:bi}) that embeds each side independently and scores by
cosine --- cheap, but hemmed in by the benchmark's anti-correlation
property (name similarity anti-predicts the label here; \S\ref{ssec:anti}) ---
and a cross-encoder (\S\ref{ssec:ce}) that reads both records together,
4--10$\times$ more expensive per pair, producing the headline
name-input result.

\subsection{Bi-encoder}
\label{ssec:bi}

We fine-tune \texttt{intfloat/e5-small-v2} (33M params, 384-dim) via
supervised contrastive learning with state features folded into the
text prompt: \texttt{"query: } $n_\ell$ \texttt{| jurisdiction: }
$c_\ell$ \texttt{| form: } $f_\ell$\texttt{"}. Training uses the
26{,}297 positives in the strict no-leakage intersection of all
three split regimes (one hard neg per anchor), MultipleNegativesRanking
loss (InfoNCE; in-batch + explicit hard negs), 3 epochs, batch 32,
AdamW lr $2\times10^{-5}$, $\sim$6 min on Apple silicon.
Ablation variants in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}: \texttt{method\_no\_hard}
(in-batch negs only), \texttt{method\_v2\_full} (adds E5-cosine
hard negs + 1{,}372 transitive positives). Loss is contrastive; the
cross-encoder below uses BCE on the logit --- different objectives by
architectural convention, not strictly controlled.

\subsection{Cross-encoder}
\label{ssec:ce}

A bi-encoder must hold each record at a fixed point in embedding
space while the event deliberately changes the record's surface
form --- it must keep cosine-close two records that look nothing
alike. A cross-encoder reads both records jointly and can compare
specific tokens between them.

We fine-tune a second-stage cross-encoder that ingests
$(\text{record}_A, \text{record}_B)$ as a single concatenated sequence
and emits a single relevance logit. The backbone is the
22M-parameter \texttt{ms-marco-\allowbreak MiniLM-L-6-v2} cross-encoder
from HuggingFace; training uses the same positives as
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} (26{,}297 plus 1{,}372 transitives) plus a 4:1 sample of E5-cosine hard
negatives per positive, with positives labeled $1.0$ and negatives
$0.0$.
Loss: standard binary cross-entropy on the logit. 2 epochs, batch 32, lr
$2\times 10^{-5}$, 100 warmup steps. The extensions in
\S\ref{ssec:main} hold architecture/optimizer constant and modify only
the serialized text, the training positives, and the backbone.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} Scoring all 125{,}519 pairs takes
$\approx$80 seconds on MPS; in deployment the cross-encoder
re-ranks a retriever's top-$K$, and the benchmark's fixed
5--10-candidate pools make direct full scoring feasible
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

\paragraph{Hard-negative re-mining.}
\label{ssec:hard_negs}
Re-mining with E5-cosine top-20 same-country candidates, in place of
the noisy token-overlap fallback, yields 652{,}764 negatives across
31{,}714 predecessors (vs.\ 253{,}733 over $\sim$20K before). The
cleaner negatives are what the cross-encoder trains on, to large
effect --- and half of what unlocks the full-record GBM's transfer
(\S\ref{ssec:anti}); for the bi-encoder they change nothing
(\S\ref{ssec:ablations}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four similarity baselines ---
\texttt{exact\_name} (normalized name equality), \texttt{token\_set}
(token Jaccard), \texttt{tfidf\_char} (char-3gram TF-IDF cosine),
and \texttt{sentence\_encoder} (zero-shot E5-small-v2, the method's
backbone un-tuned) --- and one \emph{supervised} classical matcher,
\texttt{features\_gbm}: gradient-boosted trees over twelve
string-similarity and field-agreement features in the
Fellegi--Sunter lineage~\cite{fellegisunter1969}, trained on the
released pool's no-leakage rows; the classical name-feature
default (\S\ref{ssec:anti} upgrades it to the full record). Six
zero-shot LLMs run under one protocol: one call per pair,
byte-identical prompts carrying the same name+state inputs as the
neural models (legal name, jurisdiction, legal form), a 0--10
integer rubric, \texttt{temperature=0} where exposed. Five API models from three
organizations cover the full test set (26{,}564 pairs each) ---
\textbf{Claude Haiku 4.5}, \textbf{Sonnet 4.6}, \textbf{Opus 4.7},
\textbf{Gemini 3.1 Pro}, \textbf{DeepSeek V4 Pro} (thinking
disabled) --- and \textbf{gpt-oss-20b} (a fourth organization) runs
locally on a 56/52/50-anchor sample
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results} footnotes).

Paired permutation tests on per-anchor expected Hits@1 separate
the comparisons cleanly: the L-6 beats all five API models on TIME
and EVENT at $p<10^{-4}$ ($+15$ to $+37$\,pp). On the 88-anchor
JURIS split it leads Haiku, Gemini, and DeepSeek ($p\leq 0.018$),
is within noise of Sonnet ($p=0.14$), and is indistinguishable
from Opus 4.7, which holds the higher point estimate (0.767 vs.\
0.750, $p=0.74$); the ensemble beats Opus on TIME/EVENT
($p<10^{-4}$), JURIS a coin flip. Figure~\ref{fig:llm_panel}
shows the panel: quality is strikingly vendor-heterogeneous ---
every Claude model outscores both other API vendors' frontier
models on every split, including Haiku, the cheapest tested;
\S\ref{sec:limitations} examines the elicitation-format confound
behind part of this spread.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{ssec:metrics}

We report per-anchor Hits@1/MRR and pairwise AUROC; Hits@3,
AUPRC, and threshold-tuned F1 ship with the released metrics.
Discrete scorers tie frequently, so Hits@k/MRR are exact
\emph{expectations over uniformly random tie-breaking} (a fixed
tie order is biased). Per-anchor metrics cover \emph{eligible}
anchors --- pool size $\geq 2$ with the gold present; the 37\% of
predecessors with no mined hard negative (\S\ref{sec:benchmark})
have singleton pools and are excluded (e.g., 1{,}612 of 2{,}760
EVENT-test anchors are eligible).

\subsection{Headline results}
\label{ssec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports test-set metrics across the three
split regimes for all baselines and method variants on the released
benchmark pool (\texttt{pairs.parquet}; token-overlap-mined
negatives). Headline cross-encoder Hits@1 95\% bootstrap confidence
intervals are $\pm$2.0\,pp (TIME), $\pm$2.3\,pp (EVENT), and $\pm$8.5\,pp
(JURIS, $n=88$ anchors); the jurisdiction-split gain comfortably clears
its CI band but absolute numbers on that split should be treated as
directional. We retest the headline
cross-encoder on a mechanically different candidate pool
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}) and find the gap shrinks but does not
collapse; Table~\ref{tab:v7} extends the L-6 cross-encoder to the
L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble.

\subsection{The construction cuts both ways: an anti-similarity baseline}
\label{ssec:anti}

\texttt{token\_set}, \texttt{tfidf\_char}, and
\texttt{sentence\_encoder} all land below 0.5 AUROC on the
held-out-time and held-out-event-type splits
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). The cause is mechanical: hard
negatives are deliberately name-similar to the predecessor while
positives are by construction name-different, so on this candidate
distribution name similarity is \emph{evidence against} a true
successor link. So we report the mirror image as its own baseline:
\texttt{anti-token\_set} ranks candidates by \emph{negated}
Jaccard, and on the curated pool it is strong
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; \emph{above} the single L-6
cross-encoder on time and event) because picking the least
name-similar candidate exploits the construction.

The supervised name-feature \texttt{features\_gbm} is the
exploit's learned apotheosis: it discovers the inversion plus the
miners' residual signatures, posts the best name-only scores in the
paper (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; above the ensemble at
$p<10^{-4}$ on TIME/EVENT), and collapses off-pool
(Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}; JURIS: 0.36 on
\textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF}, 0.12 fielded; AUROC 0.54--0.57 and
0.45--0.52).

Is that collapse a fact about feature models, or about what we fed
this one? Its two handicaps --- v1 negatives, name-only features
--- are removable, separately and jointly. On the cross-encoder's
own E5-mined negatives, the name-feature GBM improves cross-pool
but still dies fielded (0.25\,/\,0.25 retrieval-conditional vs.\
0.46\,/\,0.50); a TF-IDF-negative retrain behaves alike
(Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}, row 3). Given the full structured record (addresses,
previous names, liveness, registration status, dates, registrar;
the successor fields the labels derive from are excluded) but v1
negatives, it wins the released pool (0.87\,/\,0.84) yet still
fails to cross pools (0.38\,/\,0.35 on TF-IDF). Given
\emph{both}, it transfers everywhere and wins
(\texttt{features\_gbm-full}): 0.78\,/\,0.75 on-pool
($+$18\,pp over the L-6, $p<10^{-4}$; $+$8\,pp over the
ensemble), 0.78\,/\,0.75 on TF-IDF, and 0.51--0.66 fielded
retrieval-conditional, where the enriched ensemble leads by
1--3\,pp on TIME/EVENT and trails by 6\,pp on JURIS (all n.s.)
--- at negligible inference cost. Permutation importance credits
fields no name-only method sees: candidate liveness, shared
postal/city, previous-name overlap, same registrar. Nor is the
transfer a live-vs-dead prior: ablating all four
liveness/registration-status features costs 4--7\,pp and transfer
survives (0.72\,/\,0.72 on TF-IDF; 0.43\,/\,0.47
fielded-conditional, at parity with the L-6).

The cross-encoder's claim accordingly narrows to its input class:
among methods that see only names and registration state --- every
LLM, bi-encoder, string baseline, and name-feature GBM --- it is
the only one whose worst pool stays strong. The transferable
signal lives in the structured record, and no name-only method,
joint attention included, can recover fields it was never given.

The unsupervised exploit shows the same pattern at lower amplitude,
collapsing under every pool change we test, though on one event
type it outranks both cross-encoders. On
\textsc{Sylvester-TF-IDF} it collapses to chance (0.25 / 0.25 /
0.20 Hits@1) while the cross-encoders hold 0.53--0.66
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}); on the
fielded pool it drops to 9--18\% (gold-included, as plotted), a
fraction of the cross-encoder's (\S\ref{ssec:robustness}). Per event it is a
one-way bet on acquirer-renaming, and there it is genuinely hard to
beat: on the M\&A slice of the TIME split ($n{=}1{,}506$) it scores
0.741, \emph{above} both the L-6 (0.625) and the ensemble (0.711)
--- the event type the inversion is built for (the full-record
GBMs still outscore it there, 0.79--0.88). The bet is one-way: the same heuristic scores 0.11 on
pure renames and 0.35 on legal-form changes, where the
cross-encoder scores 0.90 and 0.85, and it loses pairwise AUROC to
the cross-encoder on every split (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).
Curated-pool Hits@1 alone can therefore be gamed; within the
name-input class, the cross-encoder's claim is being strong
\emph{simultaneously} on the curated pool, the alternative-miner
pool, the fielded pool, and every event type with a usable sample
(Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}). The mechanism is joint attention:
asking whether the \emph{differences} between two records look
like a lifecycle-event pattern, not how similar two strings are.

\subsection{Per-event-type breakdown}
\label{ssec:per_event}

\paragraph{Held-out time.} Pure renames
(\texttt{CHANGE\_LEGAL\_NAME}, $n=10$) are essentially solved by
every similarity-direction method including zero-shot E5-small
(0.90 Hits@1, no lift); only the inverted exploit fails them
(0.11, \S\ref{ssec:anti}).

comes from the acquirer and string similarity cannot bridge the

\label{ssec:robustness}

candidates from the same family of hard-negative miners --- v1
token-overlap and v2 E5-cosine both rank candidates by name-related signals,
and the cross-encoder trains on E5-cosine negs. The obvious question: does the gap
survive an evaluation pool built with a mechanically different
miner, or is the headline number partly an artifact of how the
negatives were chosen?

\paragraph{What the 2$\times$2 tells us.} The cross-encoder
\emph{architecture} generalizes across miners: each trained model
lands within 1--2\,pp of the other on the pool it never saw
(TIME/EVENT), miner-matching is worth a bounded $+7$--$11$\,pp,
and the gain over zero-shot E5 survives every pairing ($+13$ to
$+40$\,pp, outside any CI band). The absolute size of the gain
still depends on how the pool was mined, so the
``$+24$--$35$\,pp'' figure in the abstract should be read in that
light.

\paragraph{Fielded retrieve-then-rerank.} The miner-robustness
experiment above shifts the mining signal but still draws candidates
from a curated negative pool. A real deployment pipeline does
something different: a first-stage retriever pulls the top-K
candidates from the full entity universe, then a second-stage model
reranks. We simulate that here. The trained
\texttt{method\_v2\_full} bi-encoder retrieves top-20 candidates per
test anchor from the full 3.3M-entity GLEIF universe, restricted to
the anchor's jurisdiction (the lightest filter a production system
would apply); the cross-encoder reranks. The filter is nearly free
(1.8--4.1\% of test positives cross countries, capping recall at
$\geq$95.9\%, far above the observed 33--50\%): the bottleneck
is the embedding, not the filter.

Bi-encoder Recall@20 is 33.2 / 39.6 / 49.7\% on TIME / EVENT /
JURIS. That is the hard ceiling for any reranker. When the bi-encoder
does retrieve the true successor, the L-6 cross-encoder picks it
\#1 with 46--57\% Hits@1, the L-6$\times$L-12 ensemble reaches
53--60\%, and the full-record GBM 51--66\% (n.s.\ vs.\ the
ensemble) --- reranker differences compress once distractors look
genuinely lifecycle-plausible.
End-to-end deployment Hits@1 (recall $\times$ conditional,
un-retrieved truths counted as misses) is 15--28\% for the L-6,
17--30\% for the ensemble, and 17--33\% for the full-record GBM.
The drop from in-distribution to deployment confirms rather
than contradicts \S\ref{ssec:ce}: the bi-encoder's
fixed-embedding limit, which fails to retrieve lifecycle-spanning
successors by cosine similarity, is exactly the bottleneck the
anti-correlation analysis predicts.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{ssec:ablations}

\paragraph{State features help modestly.} Ablating jurisdiction and
legal form from the prompt costs 1.1--1.8\,pp Hits@1 on every split,
most on held-out event type and least on held-out jurisdiction.

\textbf{Explicit hard negatives slightly \emph{hurt} the
bi-encoder} (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}):
\texttt{method\_no\_hard} edges out the v1 full model by
0.4--1.1\,pp Hits@1 on every split, and E5-cosine re-mining does not
change that. The culprit is the
\texttt{token\_overlap\_same\_country} class, 92\% of mined v1
negatives and the lowest-precision one; in-batch negatives from
MultipleNegativesRanking are cleaner on their own. So better
explicit negatives do not always help contrastive training --- but
they are exactly what the cross-encoder needs
(\S\ref{ssec:hard_negs}).

\subsection{Additional diagnostics}
\label{ssec:diagnostics}

Four diagnostics; full tables ship with the released code.
\textbf{B-cubed cluster F1}~\cite{binette2024} is 0.92 / 0.90 /
0.91 for the cross-encoder vs.\ $\leq$0.48 for any bi-encoder
(which collapse to one giant cluster at the tuned threshold);
restricted to true clusters of size ${\geq}2$, a more honest
0.85 / 0.84 / 0.88.
\textbf{Calibration and selective prediction.} ECE (10-bin) 0.20,
Brier 0.18 --- over-confident in the high-score bin, and a
train-fitted temperature does not fix it, so we report uncalibrated
scores. A selective-prediction~\cite{geifman2017} sweep on the
ensemble reaches Hits@1 0.93--0.98 at 20\% coverage and
0.83--0.98 at 50\%, so high-precision users can threshold.
\textbf{Cross-domain transfer.} Zero-shot Magellan
Walmart-Amazon~\cite{magellan2016}:
the cross-encoder \emph{loses} to its own zero-shot base encoder
by 5.9\,pp AUROC. The fine-tuned representation is specialized to
lifecycle-spanning legal-entity matching, not generic ER.
\textbf{Label quality, second opinion.} A rule-based re-classifier
--- orthogonal to the primary's event-type evidence, though still
name-informed --- agrees with it on 98.7\% [95\% CI 97.0--100\%]
of the 231/277 stratified-sampled positives it judges confidently
(46 marked uncertain), bounding heuristically detectable label
noise below 2\% there; human expert review is future work.

\texttt{same\_name\_same\_country} remains a wall at
$\approx$0.89. The cross-encoder breaks it (0.89 $\to$ 0.43; 0.36

the prompt enrichment that injects GLEIF L2 parent/child names
makes it \emph{worse}. At 99 held-out examples the headline impact
is small, but the direction is clear: explicit multi-class
modeling of corporate-hierarchy relationships.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{ssec:err}

Of 100 sampled \texttt{method\_full} failures on held-out time:
66 mixed/other, 19 where anchor and true successor share \emph{no}
tokens, 8 substring-overlap distractors, 7 exact normalized-name
collisions.

The 19 ``no shared tokens'' cases are the hardest: no
similarity-based representation can solve them --- they require
world knowledge, graph context, or the full structured record
(\S\ref{ssec:anti}). Figure~\ref{fig:task}'s anchor is
representative: resolving ``DB'' to Deutsche Bank is external
knowledge a 33M-parameter encoder is unlikely to memorize from
name strings alone.

\paragraph{Residual JURIS failures cluster on sibling
consolidation.} Of the 19 / 88 anchors the ensemble mis-ranks on
JURIS, $\geq$11 are sibling consolidations (7 are ``\texttt{EPIC
<port> PTE.\ LTD.}'' vessels absorbed into one owner; the model
returns sibling vessels), plus Japanese-script renames the ASCII
tokenizer cannot bridge. Multilingual pretraining and
ownership-graph context would address both.

\subsection{Why this matters in practice}

Merchant embeddings at scale~\cite{stripe_ai}, vendor
deduplication, and compliance entity backbones all face the
lifecycle-ER gap after a merger or rebrand; \textsc{Sylvester}
lets practitioners test for it --- and \S\ref{ssec:anti} says the
cheapest fix is feeding the matcher the whole record.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{The ``anti-correlation'' finding is a construction
artifact} of mining name-similar negatives against name-different
positives (\S\ref{ssec:anti}); it motivates joint encoding here,
not a universal claim about name similarity in ER.

\paragraph{In-distribution headline overstates deployment.}
The headline Hits@1 is measured on the released pool; end-to-end on
the fielded pipeline every reranker falls to 15--33\%
(\S\ref{ssec:robustness}) because bi-encoder Recall@20 is
33--50\%, so the reranker often never sees the true successor.
The gain is partly pool-specific but survives miner shift ($+13$ to
$+39$\,pp; Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). A stronger first stage
(hybrid sparse--dense, or a pre-filtered shortlist) is the
highest-leverage future work.

\paragraph{Seed variance and a sliver of mislabeled hard negatives.}
Over seeds 42/7/1337 the bi-encoder varies $\pm 0.5/0.4/1.6$\,pp
and the enriched cross-encoder $\pm 0.5/0.3/1.6$\,pp
(TIME/EVENT/JURIS) --- JURIS's spread tracks its small sample.
Separately, $205/93{,}695 = 0.22\%$ of hard negatives appear in
GLEIF's \texttt{SuccessorEntity}; dropping them moves results
$\leq$0.2\,pp.

\paragraph{Survivorship bias and thin slices.} GLEIF
\texttt{SuccessorEntity} is voluntarily populated, biasing positives
toward well-counseled regulated entities; of 302 marquee Wikidata
M\&A pairs, 283 are absent from it (\S\ref{ssec:splits}), so
high-profile events are under-represented. The held-out-event test
set is 94.5\% absorption and the jurisdiction test has 88 eligible
anchors ($\pm$8.5\,pp CI): gains clear the CI band on all three
splits, but per-split absolutes carry those sample sizes.
Eligibility itself conditions on a name-colliding neighbour
existing, amplifying the construction (\S\ref{ssec:metrics}); a
277-pair second-classifier check bounds detectable label noise at
$<$2\% (\S\ref{ssec:diagnostics}). Splits are pair-level, not entity-disjoint
(32\%\,/\,21\% of time-split test candidates\,/\,gold
successors appear in training pairs; JURIS $<$1\%), though L-6
Hits@1 shows no seen-entity advantage (unseen 0.62 vs.\ seen 0.57
TIME; 0.56 vs.\ 0.58 EVENT, both n.s.); entity-disjoint splits are
a stricter future design.

\paragraph{LLM baselines: what the discrete scale hides.} A 0--10
rubric caps what ranking can express: models forfeit 13--48\,pp
Hits@1 to within-pool ties, and Gemini 3.1 Pro scores every
candidate identically on 55\% of TIME anchors --- oracle
tie-breaking would give it 0.72 vs.\ 0.23 measured, above our L-6
--- while Sonnet/Opus collapse only $\sim$5\% of pools and the
cross-encoder's continuous scores pay no tie tax. Prompt format
also moves results (batched Sonnet 4.6 scores 0.46 TIME vs.\ 0.40
single-pair on the same 56 anchors). The panel therefore bounds
what this elicitation format extracts, not what these models can
do: finer scales, log-probs, or comparative tie-breaks would
likely raise them --- as would a full-record prompt, the LLM
analogue of \S\ref{ssec:anti}'s finding --- and cross-vendor gaps
may partly reflect exposure to GLEIF-derived text.

\paragraph{The model is specialized, and parent/subsidiary is a
thin slice.} The cross-encoder underperforms zero-shot E5-small on
out-of-domain product matching (Walmart-Amazon, $-$5.9\,pp AUROC;
\S\ref{ssec:diagnostics}): the representation is specialized to
lifecycle ER, not generic. Symmetrically, no cross-encoder here was
given the \emph{full} structured record the winning GBM sees; the
enriched-prompt ensemble injects part of it and closes the fielded
gap, so a full-record cross-encoder is the natural next comparison. Parent/subsidiary negatives regress
(0.50 $\to$ 0.67 $\to$ 0.75 across bi-encoder, L-6, and ensemble),
though at 99 held-out examples the effect is weak; explicit
multi-class modeling of related-but-distinct entities is clear
future work.

\section{Conclusion}

\textsc{Sylvester} is an open benchmark for lifecycle-aware
entity resolution on financial entities, built entirely from
public GLEIF, SEC EDGAR, and Wikidata data, with a three-pool
protocol that separates methods that learn the pool from methods
that learn the task. Name-only exploits post the paper's best
name-input scores and collapse off-pool. Among name-input methods,
one 22M-parameter cross-encoder is the only survivor, above every
zero-shot LLM we test on the two large splits. And the ceiling is the input, not the
architecture: boosted trees over the full structured registry
record transfer across every pool with the highest minimum in the
paper, at negligible inference cost --- the lifecycle signal lives
in the record, not the name. What remains open is the first stage:
every reranker is capped by 33--50\% retrieval recall, so
candidate generation, not matching, is where lifecycle ER breaks
today.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code, parsers, predictions, trained
weights, and a datasheet: \url{https://github.com/chang-07/er-research}.
Every non-LLM result reproduces from raw GLEIF in $\sim$2 hours on
one MacBook.
